Who bears the AI shock?

Every worker AI displaces stops paying taxes and starts claiming benefits. Which workers those are swings Britain’s bill by billions — and in every scenario we simulated, inequality went up.

By Vahid Ahmadi and Mani Basharzad

Britain has one of the most AI-exposed workforces among advanced economies. If AI displaces workers, what it costs the country — in poverty, in inequality, in lost tax revenue — depends less on how many jobs go than on whose. Using PolicyEngine UK’s open-source microsimulation and Family Resources Survey microdata, we pushed a 7 per cent employment shock — derived from Goldman Sachs projections — through the full UK tax and benefit system under five rival incidence patterns drawn from the competing empirical literatures — displacement proportional to occupational AI exposure, concentrated on junior workers, compressing expertise premia, spread uniformly, and a top-loaded stress test. These are not refinements of one forecast; they are different theories of who loses, and as Figure 1 shows, the safety net treats them very differently.

Two results hold across every calibration we tested. First, measured inequality rises in all 66 combinations of displacement rates and wage uplifts (Figure 2) — even when job losses are deliberately concentrated among high earners — because surviving workers’ wage gains and the boost to capital income accrue near the top faster than means-tested benefits cushion the bottom. Nothing here hinges on the 7 per cent central case: the grid varies both the displacement rate and the wage uplift, and the finding holds in every cell. Second, incidence swings the bill by billions: as Figure 1 shows, the mean Exchequer cost ranges from around £14 billion a year under uniform incidence to £33 billion under the top-loaded stress test, while absolute poverty moves by up to around two percentage points, and the two move in opposite directions. A junior-concentrated shock is a poverty problem; a top-loaded one is a revenue problem. Policy prepared for one is unprepared for the other.

The essay would close on a margin that rarely gets priced in: whether the shock arrives as job losses or as wage cuts. In a paired comparison holding the aggregate earnings loss fixed, shifting it from redundancies to pay cuts roughly triples the fiscal cost to the Exchequer while leaving poverty almost untouched — the form of the AI shock, not just its size, decides whether it becomes a Treasury problem or a welfare problem. The piece would walk a general reader through these simulations, explain why the welfare state is an overlooked AI policy instrument, and end with what it would take to make Britain’s safety net insure the right margin before the shock arrives rather than after. The full paper is on SSRN, and all code and aggregate results are public.
