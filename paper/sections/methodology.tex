\section{Data and methods}
\label{sec:methodology}

The analysis has three stages. I attach an occupation-level measure of AI exposure to workers in a calibrated UK household microdataset, translate an assumed economy-wide shock into person-level changes to employment, wages and capital income, and pass baseline and shocked data through a tax-benefit microsimulation model.

\subsection{Measuring AI exposure}
\label{sec:exposure}

I start from the AI Occupational Exposure (AIOE) index of \citet{felten2021}, which links progress in ten AI application areas to the ability requirements of each occupation in O*NET. The AIOE is standardised across occupations (mean zero, unit standard deviation), so it takes negative values for occupations with below-average exposure. I combine it with the complementarity index $\theta_l\in[0,1]$ of \citet{pizzinelli2023}, built from O*NET work-context descriptors and job-zone requirements; high-$\theta$ occupations are those in which AI is more likely to complement than replace human judgement. The complementarity-adjusted exposure of occupation group $l$ is
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:caioe}
\text{C-AIOE}_l = \text{AIOE}_l \times \bigl(1 - (\theta_l - \theta_{\min})\bigr),
\end{equation}
where $\theta_{\min}$ is the lowest complementarity among the groups. Because the standardised AIOE can be negative, so can the C-AIOE (Table~\ref{tab:jobloss}). Before allocating job losses I therefore shift the scores so that the least-exposed group scores zero, $\widetilde{\text{C}}_l = \text{C-AIOE}_l - \min_k \text{C-AIOE}_k \ge 0$, and the allocation rule uses $\widetilde{\text{C}}_l$.

No published UK C-AIOE series exists at the level I need, so I build one. AIOE scores come from the UK SOC2020 mapping published with \citet{dsit2023}, which chains the \citet{felten2021} scores through US SOC2018, US SOC2010 and ISCO-08 to UK SOC2020. I reconstruct $\theta$ from the public O*NET 27.3 release following \citet{pizzinelli2023}; the reconstruction reproduces the published ordering up to a level offset, which does not matter because equations~\eqref{eq:caioe} and~\eqref{eq:wage} use $\theta$ only relative to its minimum and its mean. Both series are aggregated to the nine SOC2020 major groups with ASHE 2025 Table~14 employment weights, which cover 340 of the 412 unit groups. Exposure therefore varies across nine groups only, and all within-group variation is lost; the dataset's sub-major group is imputed, and I do not use it.

\subsection{Shock scenarios}
\label{sec:scenarios}

The size of an AI shock cannot be estimated from historical data, so I treat it as a scenario input. The central calibration follows the Irish design of \citet{doorley2026}: 7 per cent of employees lose their jobs and surviving employees receive an average wage uplift of 2.6 per cent. Doorley et al.\ derive these inputs from one investment-bank scenario exercise \citep{briggs2023}, which describes displacement over a roughly decade-long adoption period with reallocation. Applying the whole 7 per cent within one simulated year turns that flow into a stock, so the central case is an impact-year stress test, not an annual forecast. For scale, the OBR estimates that around \genObrFrsSubstitutionPct~per cent of the UK labour force could be exposed to substitution from AI over the next ten years, and a further \genObrFrsComplementPct~per cent could have their work complemented \citep[Box~4.1]{obr2026frs}. I also follow \citet{doorley2026} in raising the return to capital by 0.4 percentage points, from 1.005 to 1.405 per cent, which they motivate with \citet{cazzaniga2024}. Around the central case, a grid crosses displacement rates of 0--10 per cent with wage uplifts of 0--5 per cent (66 cells, capital shock on throughout; Online Appendix~\ref{app:grid}), and two presets add a 1 per cent sensitivity case with no wage gain and a 13 per cent stress case.

\subsection{Implementing the shock at the micro level}
\label{sec:shocks}

\paragraph{Employment.} The number displaced is the displacement rate times weighted employees. It is allocated across groups in proportion to employment times shifted exposure:
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:jobloss}
\text{JobLoss}_l = \text{TotalJobLoss} \times \frac{\text{EMP}_l\, \widetilde{\text{C}}_l}{\sum_{k} \text{EMP}_k\, \widetilde{\text{C}}_k}.
\end{equation}
Table~\ref{tab:jobloss} reports the resulting quota rate for each group. The rule concentrates losses in administrative and secretarial, managerial, professional and associate professional occupations, and elementary occupations, the least-exposed group, receive no displacement. Employees without a recorded group form a pseudo-group with the employment-weighted mean exposure. Within each group, systematic sampling on a random permutation selects the displaced: every record in a group has the same inclusion probability (up to prescribed tilts such as the youth multiplier), the quota holds in expectation, and a record's calibrated weight enters only through the quota accounting. A displaced worker's employment income, hours, pension contributions (including salary sacrifice) and statutory pay are set to zero and their status to unemployed; dual jobholders keep self-employment income. PolicyEngine UK takes no spell-duration input, so displaced workers are assessed as unemployed for the whole year. The self-employed are outside both labour-market shocks, following \citet{doorley2026}.

\paragraph{Wages.} Each surviving employee receives an uplift proportional to complementarity:
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:wage}
\text{WageChange}_l = \text{WageGrowth} \times \frac{\theta_l}{\bar{\theta}} \times \text{Wage}_l,
\qquad
\bar{\theta} = \frac{\sum_l \text{EMP}_l \, \theta_l}{\sum_l \text{EMP}_l},
\end{equation}
with $\text{EMP}_l$ baseline employment. The employment-weighted mean uplift equals the assumed growth rate. Two features follow from the rule. Higher-$\theta$ occupations earn more, so the wage bill grows slightly faster than the headline rate, and survivors have above-average $\theta$, so displacement raises the realised mean uplift a little. Non-displaced workers can only gain; Online Appendix~\ref{app:ripple} prices displaced workers bidding down others' wages in reduced form.

\paragraph{Capital.} Savings interest and dividend income are scaled by $1.405/1.005$, a \genCapitalScalePct~per cent increase; rental income and returns inside pensions are unchanged. The baseline return of 1.005 per cent comes from the Irish calibration. The same 0.4-point shock on a 4 per cent base would raise capital incomes by 10 per cent, so the channel is best read directly as a \genCapitalScalePct~per cent proportional increase. Section~\ref{sec:results-closure} replaces it with an explicit account of where the displaced wage bill goes.

\subsection{Incidence and adjustment-margin scenarios}
\label{sec:incidence}

Holding the central aggregate shock fixed, I reallocate the same expected number of displaced workers under five displacement rules. Survivor wages and capital income are held fixed at the person level, so the comparisons isolate who loses the job.
\begin{description}
\item[Exposure-proportional.] Equation~\eqref{eq:jobloss}; the central case and the rule behind the grid.
\item[Junior-concentrated.] Draws tilted towards workers aged 16--24 by a multiplier of 1.29, the ratio of junior to overall employment falls in press-release figures for maximally exposed UK firms from \citet{kleinteeselink2025} (5.8/4.5), within unchanged group quotas.
\item[Expertise-compression.] Displacement probabilities doubled (an author choice) for the top weighted-earnings tertile of workers in above-median-exposure occupations: a high-earner displacement tilt named after the hypothesis that motivates it \citep{autor2024, hosseinilichtinger2026barriers}, whose formal mechanisms work through wages rather than job loss.
\item[Uniform.] Every employee has the same displacement probability.
\item[Top-loaded stress test.] Probability proportional to shifted exposure times a junior factor of 1.29 and wage-tier factors of 9.6 above and 0.6 below weighted-median person earnings. The tier factors carry press-release figures for maximally exposed UK firms (employment falls of 9.6 per cent in high-paying firms and almost none in low-paying ones) over to persons, using person earnings as a proxy for firm pay \citep{kleinteeselink2025}. I treat this family as an upper stress case, not an estimated schedule.
\end{description}

\paragraph{Wage margin.} A separate family moves the same earnings loss to wage cuts \citep{acemoglu2022tasks}. No one loses a job; every employee's earnings fall by an occupation-level percentage proportional to $\widetilde{\text{C}}_l$, scaled so that the aggregate gross loss equals the realised loss of the paired central displacement draw (\genGrossCutSharePct~per cent of the baseline employee wage bill at seed~0, above 7 per cent because group job-loss rates rise with group pay). The 2.6 per cent uplift and the capital shock apply on top. A second gradient uses the potential-supply-shift measure of \citet{hosseinilichtinger2026barriers} to rank the cuts. A mixed family thins the central displacement draw to a share $\lambda\in\{0,0.25,0.5,0.75,1\}$ and fills the gap to the same gross loss with C-AIOE-graded cuts, and a survivor-wage panel holds the central draw fixed while the survivor-wage effect runs from $-5$ to $+5$ per cent.

\subsection{Microsimulation with PolicyEngine UK}
\label{sec:microsim}

I use PolicyEngine UK 2.174.1 (policyengine.py 6.16.1 release bundle) on Microcosm UK 2024--25 (build \nolinkurl{microcosm-uk-2024-25-national-20261107T020434Z-eeb6093e}). Microcosm starts from the Family Resources Survey 2024--25 (UK Data Service SN~9563), adds records drawn from the HMRC Survey of Personal Incomes (SPI) for top incomes, imputes variables the survey does not collect, and calibrates household weights to 1{,}514 administrative and survey targets, including SPI income by band up to \pounds 1 million, ONS occupational employment, DWP caseloads and OBR receipts; 98.7 per cent of targets fall within 10 per cent.\footnote{Target counts and fit are from the build's calibration diagnostics in the Microcosm repository.} The file holds \genDataHouseholds~households and \genDataPersons~persons, grossing to \genDataPopulationM~million people and \genDataEmployeesM~million employees in 2026; \genDataEmployeesSocPct~per cent of weighted employees have a recorded SOC2020 major group, carried from the FRS and imputed from income for SPI-drawn records. The simulation year is 2026.

Each scenario runs a baseline and a shocked simulation on identical records, so reported effects contain no resampling noise, but they inherit the survey's sampling error. Income results use HBAI cash disposable income. Absolute poverty lines are fixed at baseline values; relative lines are 60 per cent of the shocked median. The Gini is computed on equivalised household income with negative incomes set to zero. The Exchequer cost is the fall in PolicyEngine's government balance, which includes income tax, employee and employer National Insurance (NI) and benefit spending; a negative cost is a net gain. Household consumption is held at baseline, so the balance carries no VAT or other indirect-tax response, and the pay bill saved when a public-sector employee is displaced is outside the balance; I report that pay bill (earnings, employer NI and employer pension contributions of displaced public-sector employees, identified by the FRS employment sector) beside the cost. The household basis counts only the items between household market income and disposable income that fall on the household: income tax, employee NI and benefits, which excludes employer NI. Its share of a market-income change is the income stabilisation coefficient of \citet{dolls2012}.

\paragraph{Draws and uncertainty.} The identity of the displaced is random. Calibration makes Microcosm's weights uneven: the \genDataEmployeeRows~employee records have a Kish effective sample size of about \genDataEmployeeKishN, so the realised number displaced varies around the quota (\genCentralMcDisplacedMinM--\genCentralMcDisplacedMaxM~million over 50 central draws). I report 50-draw means for the central case, the incidence families and the policy reforms; 20-draw means for the channel split, the capital-income coverage and capital variants, the public-sector pay bill, the OBR benchmark and the duration and take-up sensitivities; and single draws at seed~0 where only one draw exists (the grid, the presets other than the central case, the wage-margin and mixed tables, and the index and ripple sensitivities), labelled as such. A value written ``$a \pm b$'' is a mean and the standard deviation across assignment draws, which describes how much a single draw can vary; it is not a confidence interval. The Monte Carlo standard error of a 50-draw mean is that standard deviation divided by $\sqrt{50}$: \pounds\genCentralMcCostSeBn~billion for the central cost. Neither number reflects survey sampling or parameter uncertainty. Appendix~\ref{app:seeds} lists the basis of every result.
