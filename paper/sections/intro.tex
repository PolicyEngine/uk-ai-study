\section{Introduction}

This paper estimates how much of an AI-driven labour-market shock the UK tax-benefit system absorbs and who bears the rest. The answer turns on two things the size of the shock does not settle: which workers lose earnings, and whether they lose their jobs or part of their pay. The occupations that score highest on AI exposure indices---professional, associate professional, administrative and managerial work---pay well and carry much of the UK's income tax and NI base \citep{felten2021, eloundou2023, dsit2023, henseke2025}, while UC and other means-tested benefits respond mainly to losses lower down the distribution. The same aggregate shock can therefore cost the Exchequer a little or a lot, and raise poverty a little or a lot, depending on where it lands.

The evidence on where it will land is unsettled. Exposure-based scenarios place the risk on highly paid cognitive work \citep{felten2021, eloundou2023}; an expertise-compression view expects AI to erode the premia of experienced workers \citep{autor2024, hosseinilichtinger2026barriers}; and early empirical studies find larger employment effects for junior workers in exposed occupations and firms \citep{kleinteeselink2025, hosseini2026, brynjolfsson2025}. The adjustment margin is equally open: in task-based models displacement can show up as relative wage declines rather than job loss \citep{acemoglu2022tasks}.

I run an AI labour-market shock through the UK tax-benefit system at household level and vary both dimensions while holding the shock's size fixed. Using PolicyEngine UK on Microcosm, a calibrated microdataset built from the Family Resources Survey 2024--25, I attach occupation-level exposure to workers and adopt the central conventions of an Irish study \citep{doorley2026}: 7 per cent of employees displaced, a 2.6 per cent wage uplift for survivors and a higher return to capital. Two comparisons organise the results.

\paragraph{Who loses the job.} Reallocating the same expected number of displaced workers across four stylised rules changes the annual Exchequer cost from \pounds\genIncUniformCostMeanBn~billion under uniform incidence to \pounds\genIncCompressionCostMeanBn~billion when displacement tilts towards high earners in exposed occupations (50-draw means); a top-loaded stress test reaches \pounds\genIncKleinCostMeanBn~billion. Poverty does not follow cost: uniform incidence costs least but raises absolute poverty and the Gini by more than exposure-proportional incidence.

\paragraph{How the loss happens.} Delivering the same gross earnings loss as occupation-level wage cuts rather than job losses raises the cost from \pounds\genWcJobCostMeanBn~billion to \pounds\genWcWageCostMeanBn~billion and lowers the rise in absolute poverty from \genWcJobPovBhcMeanPp~to \genWcWagePovBhcMeanPp~points (means over \genWcDraws~paired draws). The Exchequer absorbs \genAbsJobGovPct~pence of each pound of earnings lost through job loss, \genAbsJobGovJsaPct~pence if every displaced worker claims UC and new-style JSA, and \genAbsCutGovPct~pence of each pound lost through wage cuts.

The exposure-proportional central case is one cell of this design. It costs \pounds\genCentralMcCostMeanBn~billion a year as the net of a \pounds\genChannelMcDispCostAbsBn~billion displacement cost and extra receipts from the survivor-wage and capital channels, so its sign reverses with a survivor-wage effect of \genBeWagePct~per cent, a capital-income uplift of about \genBeCapInterpUpliftPct~per cent or out-of-work spells of about \genBeDurMonths~months. An explicit account of where the displaced wage bill goes, with corporation tax and an assumption about the UK-household ownership of profits, gives results from a net gain of \pounds\genFaClosedPhiOneShareOneAbsBn~billion to a cost of \pounds\genFaClosedPhiHalfShareHalfAbsBn~billion. The OBR's own technological-displacement scenario, in which equilibrium unemployment rises from \genObrUnempBasePct~to \genObrUnempScenPct~per cent, adds an average of \pounds\genObrBorrowingBn~billion a year to borrowing \citep{obr2026}; displacing employees on the same head-count scale, with flat earnings and no capital gain, costs \pounds\genObrCostMeanBn~billion in the simulation year, almost all of it lost income tax and NI.

The paper contributes to three literatures. For the AI exposure literature it supplies the household tax-benefit layer that exposure indices and macroeconomic scenarios lack; I am not aware of a published study that runs an AI shock through the UK tax-benefit system at household level. For the literature on automatic stabilisers \citep{dolls2012} it compares the absorption of an extensive-margin and an intensive-margin shock of equal size. For the public finance of AI \citep{korinek2026, obr2026frs} it shows, household by household, how a shift from labour income to profits plays out under current UK rules. The code and aggregate results are public;\footnote{\url{https://github.com/PolicyEngine/uk-ai-study}.} exact regeneration requires access to Microcosm.
