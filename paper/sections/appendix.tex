\subsection{Seed and draw conventions}
\label{app:seeds}

Displacement is a random draw of which employees lose their jobs, subject to occupation-group quotas. Within each draw, ordering keys are uniform within the group, so a record's inclusion probability does not depend on its calibrated weight. The results rest on the following bases.
\begin{itemize}
\item Means over 50 paired draws (seeds 0--49): the central scenario (Table~\ref{tab:presets}) and its cost by component, the five displacement rules (Table~\ref{tab:incidence}), the policy reforms (Table~\ref{tab:policy}), the decile transition and wage-gain profiles, and the regression slope used to adjust the index comparison.
\item Means over 20 paired draws (seeds 0--19), with identical displacement in every run of a seed: the channel split and cost per displaced worker, the dividend-coverage runs, the capital variants (Table~\ref{tab:capvariants}) and the capital break-even, the public-sector pay bill, the duration and take-up sensitivities, the 20-draw decile profile (Figure~\ref{fig:decomp-ci}) and the gender displacement rates. The OBR benchmark (Tables~\ref{tab:stabilisation} and~\ref{tab:obr}) uses the same seeds at a lower displacement rate, so it pairs with the other runs by seed, not by person.
\item Single draws at seed~0: the low and high presets, the shock-size grid and its variants, the wage-margin and mixed-margin tables, the survivor-wage panel and its break-even, the factor-income account, the absorption rates, the decile decomposition (Figure~\ref{fig:decomp}), the incidence figure, the index and ripple sensitivities and the caseloads. Seed~0 ranks \genSeedZeroRankPovBhc{} of 50 on the BHC poverty change and \genSeedZeroRankCost{} on cost among the exposure-proportional draws, so its cost sits near the mean and its poverty change near the bottom of the range.
\item Means over five draws (seeds 0--4): the 2008--09 backcast; \genDurReformDraws{} draws: the duration-consistent policy ranking.
\end{itemize}
A value written $a \pm b$ is a mean and the standard deviation across assignment draws; the standard error of the mean is $b/\sqrt{n}$ for $n$ draws.

\subsection{The shock-size grid}
\label{app:grid}

To move beyond the presets I simulate a grid of 66 scenarios, crossing eleven displacement rates (0 to 10 per cent of employees, allocated in proportion to exposure, as in the presets) with six wage uplifts (0 to 5 per cent), each combined with the capital income channel, all under exposure-proportional incidence. Figure~\ref{fig:griddisp} maps the change in average household disposable income. The corners bracket the range: with a 5 per cent wage gain and no job loss, average disposable income changes by $\genGridDispMaxPct$ per cent; with 10 per cent displacement and no wage gain it changes by $\genGridDispMinPct$ per cent. % results/jr16/grid.csv
The capital channel shifts the whole surface upwards: on its own (no displacement, no wage gain) it raises average disposable income by $\genGridCapOnlyDispPct$ per cent. With no wage growth, average disposable income still rises at 3 per cent displacement ($\genGridDispThreeZeroPct$ per cent) and first falls at 4 per cent ($\genGridDispFourZeroPct$ per cent); at the central 7 per cent displacement rate it falls with up to 4 per cent wage growth ($\genGridDispSevenFourPct$ per cent) and rises slightly with 5 per cent ($\genGridDispSevenFivePct$ per cent). Whether AI raises or lowers average living standards in this grid therefore depends on the balance between displacement and wage growth, and on the assumed capital-return shock, all of which are uncertain.
\begin{figure}[htbp]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=.85\textwidth]{../results/jr16/fig4_5_disposable_grid.png}
\caption{Change in average household disposable income (per cent) across the 66-cell grid of displacement rates and wage uplifts (single draw, seed 0).}
\label{fig:griddisp}
\end{figure}

The grid's fiscal measure is narrower than the government balance behind the headline cost of Section~\ref{sec:results-channels}: it is income tax plus National Insurance minus cash benefit expenditure as simulated on the household microdata, without employer National Insurance. Over the 50 central draws the narrow cash basis shows a net gain of \pounds\genCostNarrowCashAbsBn~billion, against a cost of \pounds\genCostTotalAbsBn~billion on the government balance; the gap of \pounds\genCostBasisGapAbsBn~billion matches the employer National Insurance component of \pounds\genCostEmployerNiAbsBn~billion, so the two measures should not be read off the same surface. On the narrow basis the grid spans a gain of \pounds\genGridRevGainBn~billion (5 per cent wage growth, no displacement) to a loss of \pounds\genGridRevLossBn~billion in the worst cell (10 per cent displacement, no wage growth). The capital shock alone adds \pounds\genGridCapOnlyRevBn~billion of net revenue. At the central displacement rate, net revenue falls by \pounds\genGridRevSevenZeroAbsBn~billion with no wage offset and rises by \pounds\genGridRevSevenThreeAbsBn~billion with a 3 per cent wage uplift. % results/jr16/grid.csv

Figure~\ref{fig:gridgini} maps the change in the Gini coefficient across the same grid. The Gini rises in all \genGridCells{} cells, from $\genGridGiniMinSignedPp$ percentage points in the capital-only cell (no displacement, no wage gain) to $\genGridGiniMaxSignedPp$ points at the far corner. The capital channel alone accounts for a substantial part of the rise, because the uplift accrues mainly to the top decile; displacement adds to it by pushing affected households down the distribution, and the wage uplift by lifting surviving workers further from workless and low-income households. Because each cell is a single draw on uneven weights, the Gini change is not strictly monotone in the displacement rate; the cross-draw spread reported for the central scenario (Section~\ref{sec:results-channels}) indicates the scale of that noise. Within the model, inequality rises even though the displacement shock lands mainly on higher earners.

\subsection{Ripple-effect sensitivity}
\label{app:ripple}

The displacement scenarios of the main text hold non-displaced wages at
the uplifted baseline, so the ripple effects of
\citet{acemoglu2022tasks}---displaced workers bidding down the wages of
the non-displaced---are absent. Here I price that channel in reduced
form. After the central displacement draw, each displaced worker's
labour supply is routed across destination SOC2020 major groups using a
row-stochastic $9\times 9$ routing matrix with a zero diagonal: the
preferred source for this matrix, a pairwise retraining-cost matrix from
the \citet{hosseinilichtinger2026barriers} data release, does not exist
(the release contains only occupation-level measures, with no
origin--destination pairs), so I fall back on employment-proportional
routing, with each origin row proportional to destination employment
shares from the same ASHE 2025 employment weights used throughout the
paper. For each destination group I compute the proportional
labour-supply inflow $s_l$ (routed displaced FTE over baseline
employment), and every non-displaced employee in group $l$ takes a wage
cut of $\eta \times s_l$ of baseline earnings, composed additively with
the $+2.6$ per cent uplift. The parameter $\eta$ is an \emph{assumed
inverse-demand slope}---the proportional wage decline per unit
proportional labour-supply inflow---not a sourced own-wage elasticity,
and no conclusion about whether the central scenario is conservative
should be drawn from any single value of it; with 7 per cent of workers displaced the inflow
shares are around 7 per cent, so $\eta = 0.3$ implies wage cuts of
roughly 2 per cent across the non-displaced workforce. This is a
directional sensitivity, not an implementation of the
\citet{acemoglu2022tasks} propagation matrix: there is no task
structure, no equilibrium feedback, and the routing is exogenous.
Table~\ref{tab:ripple} reports the results for $\eta \in \{0, 0.15,
0.3, 0.5\}$.

Under this specification, the ripple channel loads mainly on the Exchequer. At $\eta =
0.3$ the fiscal cost rises by \pounds \genRippleCostRiseBn~billion, from \pounds \genCentralCostBn{} to
\pounds \genCentralRippleCostBn~billion, because thin, widespread wage cuts across the
non-displaced workforce forgo income tax and National Insurance at the
margin. The distributional estimates move less: BHC poverty rises by
\genRipplePovExtraPp~points more than in the central run (Table~\ref{tab:ripple} gives the Gini change); the cuts are imposed on employed workers concentrated in
the middle and upper deciles. Adding this particular ripple raises the
simulated fiscal cost; other routing and wage-response assumptions could
produce different results.
