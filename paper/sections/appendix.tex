\subsection{Seed and draw conventions}
\label{app:seeds}

Displacement is a random draw of which employees lose their jobs, subject to occupation-group quotas. Within each draw, ordering keys are uniform within the group, so a record's inclusion probability does not depend on its calibrated weight. Each result rests on one of the following bases.

Means over 50 paired draws (seeds 0--49) underlie the central scenario (Table~\ref{tab:presets}) and its cost by component, the five displacement rules (Table~\ref{tab:incidence} and the right panel of Figure~\ref{fig:incidence}), the policy reforms (Table~\ref{tab:policy}), the decile transition and wage-gain profiles, and the regression slope used to adjust the index comparison.

Means over 20 paired draws (seeds 0--19), with identical displacement in every run of a seed, underlie the channel split and cost per displaced worker, the dividend-coverage runs, the capital variants (Table~\ref{tab:capvariants}) and the capital break-even, the public-sector pay bill, the duration and take-up sensitivities, the take-up = 1 and new-style JSA variants of the central scenario, the margin comparison (Table~\ref{tab:wagemargin}, except its PSS row) and the absorption of wage cuts, the 20-draw decile profile (Online Appendix Figure~\ref{fig:decomp-ci}) and the gender displacement rates. The OBR benchmark (Tables~\ref{tab:stabilisation} and~\ref{tab:obr}) uses the same seeds at a lower displacement rate, so it pairs with the other runs by seed, not by person; its take-up = 1 and new-style JSA variants pair with it person by person.

Single draws at seed~0 underlie the low and high presets, the shock-size grid and its variants, the PSS-gradient wage cuts and the mixed-margin table, the survivor-wage panel and its break-even, the factor-income account, the household-basis absorption of job loss and its take-up = 1 and new-style JSA variants, the decile decomposition (Figure~\ref{fig:decomp}), the left panel of Figure~\ref{fig:incidence}, the index and ripple sensitivities and the caseloads. Seed~0 ranks \genSeedZeroRankPovBhc{} of 50 on the BHC poverty change and \genSeedZeroRankCost{} on cost among the exposure-proportional draws, so its cost sits near the mean and its poverty change near the bottom of the range.

Means over five draws (seeds 0--4) underlie the 2008--10 backcast, and means over \genDurReformDraws{} draws underlie the duration-consistent policy ranking.

A value written $a \pm b$ is a mean and the standard deviation across assignment draws; the standard error of the mean is $b/\sqrt{n}$ for $n$ draws.

\subsection{The shock-size grid}
\label{app:grid}

To move beyond the presets I simulate a grid of 66 scenarios, crossing eleven displacement rates (0 to 10 per cent of employees, allocated in proportion to exposure, as in the presets) with six wage uplifts (0 to 5 per cent), each combined with the capital income channel, all under exposure-proportional incidence. Figure~\ref{fig:griddisp} maps the change in average household disposable income. The corners bracket the range: with a 5 per cent wage gain and no job loss, average disposable income changes by $\genGridDispMaxPct$ per cent; with 10 per cent displacement and no wage gain it changes by $\genGridDispMinPct$ per cent. % results/jr16/grid.csv
The capital channel shifts the whole surface upwards: on its own (no displacement, no wage gain) it raises average disposable income by $\genGridCapOnlyDispPct$ per cent. With no wage growth, average disposable income still rises at 3 per cent displacement ($\genGridDispThreeZeroPct$ per cent) and first falls at 4 per cent ($\genGridDispFourZeroPct$ per cent); at the central 7 per cent displacement rate it falls with up to 4 per cent wage growth ($\genGridDispSevenFourPct$ per cent) and rises with 5 per cent ($\genGridDispSevenFivePct$ per cent). Whether AI raises or lowers average living standards in this grid therefore depends on the balance between displacement and wage growth, and on the assumed capital-return shock, all of which are uncertain.
The grid's fiscal measure is narrower than the government balance behind the headline cost of Section~\ref{sec:results-channels}: it is income tax plus National Insurance minus cash benefit expenditure as simulated on the household microdata, without employer National Insurance. Over the 50 central draws the narrow cash basis shows a net gain of \pounds\genCostNarrowCashAbsBn~billion, against a cost of \pounds\genCostTotalAbsBn~billion on the government balance; the gap of \pounds\genCostBasisGapAbsBn~billion matches the employer National Insurance component of \pounds\genCostEmployerNiAbsBn~billion, so the two measures should not be read off the same surface. On the narrow basis the grid spans a gain of \pounds\genGridRevGainBn~billion (5 per cent wage growth, no displacement) to a loss of \pounds\genGridRevLossBn~billion in the worst cell (10 per cent displacement, no wage growth). The capital shock alone adds \pounds\genGridCapOnlyRevBn~billion of net revenue. At the central displacement rate, net revenue falls by \pounds\genGridRevSevenZeroAbsBn~billion with no wage offset and rises by \pounds\genGridRevSevenThreeAbsBn~billion with a 3 per cent wage uplift. % results/jr16/grid.csv

Figure~\ref{fig:gridgini} maps the change in the Gini coefficient across the same grid. The Gini rises in all \genGridCells{} cells, from $\genGridGiniMinSignedPp$ percentage points in the capital-only cell (no displacement, no wage gain) to $\genGridGiniMaxSignedPp$ points at the far corner. The capital-only cell shows the capital channel's contribution, which arises because the uplift accrues mainly to the top decile; displacement adds to it by pushing affected households down the distribution, and the wage uplift by lifting surviving workers further from workless and low-income households. Because each cell is a single draw on uneven weights, the Gini change is not strictly monotone in the displacement rate; the cross-draw spread reported for the central scenario (Section~\ref{sec:results-channels}) indicates the scale of that noise. Within the model, inequality rises even though the displacement shock lands mainly on higher earners.

\subsection{Ripple-effect sensitivity}
\label{app:ripple}

The displacement scenarios of the main text hold non-displaced wages at
the uplifted baseline, so the ripple effects of
\citet{acemoglu2022tasks}---displaced workers bidding down the wages of
the non-displaced---are absent. Here I price that channel in reduced
form. After the central displacement draw, each displaced worker's
labour supply is routed across destination SOC2020 major groups using a
row-stochastic $9\times 9$ routing matrix with a zero diagonal: the
preferred source for this matrix, a pairwise retraining-cost matrix from
the \citet{hosseinilichtinger2026barriers} data release, does not exist
(the release contains only occupation-level measures, with no
origin--destination pairs), so I fall back on employment-proportional
routing, with each origin row proportional to destination employment
shares from the same ASHE 2025 employment weights used throughout the
paper. For each destination group I compute the proportional
labour-supply inflow $s_l$ (routed displaced full-time-equivalent workers over baseline
employment), and every non-displaced employee in group $l$ takes a wage
cut of $\eta \times s_l$ of baseline earnings, composed additively with
the $+2.6$ per cent uplift. The parameter $\eta$ is an \emph{assumed
inverse-demand slope}---the proportional wage decline per unit
proportional labour-supply inflow---not a sourced own-wage elasticity,
and no conclusion about whether the central scenario is conservative
should be drawn from any single value of it; with 7 per cent of workers displaced the inflow
shares are around 7 per cent, so $\eta = 0.3$ implies wage cuts of
roughly 2 per cent across the non-displaced workforce. This is a
directional sensitivity, not an implementation of the
\citet{acemoglu2022tasks} propagation matrix: there is no task
structure, no equilibrium feedback, and the routing is exogenous.
Table~\ref{tab:ripple} reports the results for $\eta \in \{0, 0.15,
0.3, 0.5\}$.

Under this specification, the ripple channel loads mainly on the Exchequer. At $\eta =
0.3$ the fiscal cost rises by \pounds \genRippleCostRiseBn~billion to
\pounds \genCentralRippleCostBn~billion, because wage cuts of about 2 per cent across the
non-displaced workforce forgo income tax and National Insurance at the
margin. The distributional estimates move less: BHC poverty rises by
\genRipplePovExtraPp~points more than in the central run (Table~\ref{tab:ripple} gives the Gini change); the cuts are imposed on employed workers concentrated in
the middle and upper deciles. Adding this particular ripple raises the
simulated fiscal cost; other routing and wage-response assumptions could
produce different results.

\subsection{A benchmark against the OBR}
\label{sec:results-obr}

The OBR's March 2026 outlook includes a technological-displacement scenario in which equilibrium unemployment rises to \genObrUnempScenPct~per cent, against \genObrUnempBasePct~per cent in the central forecast for 2030; higher productivity leaves GDP unchanged and real earnings do not rise, so the labour share falls and the profit share rises \citep[Box~2.2]{obr2026}. Borrowing is higher than in the central forecast by an average of \pounds\genObrBorrowingBn~billion a year: receipts are \pounds\genObrReceiptsLostBn~billion lower, from lower personal tax and VAT net of higher corporation tax, welfare spending is \pounds\genObrWelfareAddedBn~billion higher, and debt interest rises with the extra borrowing \citep[para.~6.18]{obr2026}.

I simulate the closest equivalent the household model allows. Displacement adds \genObrUnempRisePp~points of Microcosm's \genObrLabourForceM~million labour force to unemployment, which is \genObrRatePct~per cent of employees under exposure-proportional incidence, with no survivor-wage change and no capital shock (\genObrDraws~draws). Table~\ref{tab:obr} sets the simulated change in the government balance beside the OBR's lines. The simulation costs \pounds\genObrCostMeanBn~billion, about \pounds\genObrCostPerDisplacedGbp{} per displaced worker. Its receipts line, \pounds\genObrReceiptsBn~billion, exceeds the OBR's \pounds\genObrReceiptsLostBn~billion, and its welfare line, \pounds\genObrWelfareBn~billion, falls short of the OBR's \pounds\genObrWelfareAddedBn~billion. Per head, the OBR's welfare addition is about \pounds\genObrWelfarePerUnempGbp{} for each additional unemployed person, against \pounds\genTkUnempUcPerPersonGbp{} of UC per unemployed person among Microcosm's existing unemployed. UC rises by \pounds\genObrUcPerDisplacedGbp{} per displaced worker in the simulation and by \pounds\genTkObrTakeupUcPerDisplacedGbp{} with full take-up; the gap to the existing unemployed reflects the displaced workers' capital, their partners' earnings and their housing tenure (Section~\ref{sec:results-deciles}). With full take-up the simulation costs \pounds\genTkObrTakeupCostMeanBn~billion, and with new-style JSA as well \pounds\genTkObrJsaCostMeanBn~billion.

The two sets of numbers measure different things in four ways. First, timing: the OBR figures average the forecast years, over which the unemployment gap builds up as the central forecast falls back to its equilibrium rate by 2030, whereas the simulation applies the full \genObrUnempRisePp-point rise in one year, so the OBR's average spans years in which the gap is still opening and is smaller than its effect once the full rise is in place. Second, coverage: the OBR's receipts include VAT on lower consumption and corporation tax on higher profits, and its borrowing includes debt interest; the simulation has neither consumption nor profit responses. Third, the OBR's welfare figure covers all welfare spending, including the contributory benefits that the central simulation does not pay to the newly displaced. Fourth, the OBR's scenario does not allocate unemployment across occupations, whereas exposure-proportional incidence displaces higher earners, whose income tax and NI make up most of the simulated cost. The long-term counterpart in the OBR's fiscal risks report, in which the wage share of GDP halves by 2075--76, lowers tax receipts by nearly 4 percentage points of GDP because wages and salaries carry an average effective tax rate of 46 per cent against 27 per cent on profits \citep[Box~4.1]{obr2026frs}.
