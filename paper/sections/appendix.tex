\subsection{Seed and draw conventions}
\label{app:seeds}

Displacement is a random draw of which employees lose their jobs, subject to occupation-group quotas. Within each draw, ordering keys are uniform within the group, so a record's inclusion probability does not depend on its calibrated weight. The results rest on the following bases.
\begin{itemize}
\item Means over 50 paired draws (seeds 0--49): the central scenario (Table~\ref{tab:presets}) and its cost by component, the five displacement rules (Table~\ref{tab:incidence}), the policy reforms (Table~\ref{tab:policy}), the decile transition and wage-gain profiles, and the regression slope used to adjust the index comparison.
\item Means over 20 paired draws (seeds 0--19), with identical displacement in every run of a seed: the channel split and cost per displaced worker, the capital-income coverage runs, the capital break-even, and the duration and take-up sensitivities; also the 20-draw decile profile and the gender displacement rates.
\item Single draws at seed~0: the low and high presets, the shock-size grid and its variants, the wage-margin and mixed-margin tables, the survivor-wage panel and its break-even, the factor-income account, the absorption rates, the index and ripple sensitivities, the caseloads, the poverty decomposition and the age shares. Seed~0 ranks \genSeedZeroRankPovBhc{} of 50 on the BHC poverty change and \genSeedZeroRankCost{} on cost among the exposure-proportional draws, so its cost sits near the mean and its poverty change near the bottom of the range.
\item Means over five draws (seeds 0--4): the 2008--09 backcast; \genDurReformDraws{} draws: the duration-consistent policy ranking.
\end{itemize}
A value written $a \pm b$ is a mean and the standard deviation across assignment draws; the standard error of the mean is $b/\sqrt{n}$ for $n$ draws.

\subsection{A historical benchmark: a 2008--09-sized employment shock}
\label{app:backcast}

The backcast displaces 2.6 per cent of employees uniformly, matching the rise in the LFS unemployment rate over 2008--2010, with no wage or capital channel (means over five draws). Besides the absolute BHC change reported in Section~\ref{sec:results-channels}, it changes absolute AHC poverty by $\genBackcastPovAhcPp$ points, relative BHC poverty by $\genBackcastRelPovPp$ points and the Gini by $\genBackcastGiniPp$ points. Recorded HBAI outturns over 2007/08--2010/11 show absolute BHC poverty and the Gini roughly flat to slightly falling and relative poverty falling, because benefits were uprated with high inflation while median incomes fell, and because unemployment spells were of mixed length. The gap measures what the excluded channels (uprating, duration, behavioural and macro-policy responses) absorbed in practice. Per point of uniform displacement the backcast gives $\genBackcastPerPointPp$ points of BHC poverty, against $\genUniformPerPointPp$ for the uniform 7 per cent family, which also carries the wage and capital channels.

\subsection{Household mediation by decile}
\label{app:mediation}

The tax-benefit system converts each market-income loss into a smaller disposable-income loss; Figure~\ref{fig:decomp} decomposes that mediation by decile into market income, benefits, and taxes and contributions, all expressed relative to baseline disposable income. The decomposition is household-level and additive: market income change plus benefit change minus tax change equals the disposable income change up to an explicit residual, which collects perimeter items inside the HBAI concept (council tax and pension deductions) and never exceeds \genDecompResidualMaxPct~per cent of baseline income in any decile.

The capital channel reshapes this mediation. In the seed-0 draw the largest disposable-income loss falls in decile~\genDecompWorstDecile: market income changes by $\genDecompMarketWorstSignedPct$ per cent of baseline disposable income, benefits by $\genDecompBenWorstSignedPct$ per cent and lower taxes and contributions return $\genDecompTaxWorstSignedPct$ per cent, leaving, with the HBAI perimeter items, a disposable-income change of $\genDecompDispWorstSignedPct$ per cent. Benefits and lower taxes and contributions offset about \genDecompAbsorbedTbWorstPct~per cent of the market loss, and \genDecompAbsorbedWorstPct~per cent including the perimeter items. In the top decile the capital uplift offsets the earnings lost by displaced workers: market income changes by $\genDecompMarketDTenSignedPct$ per cent and disposable income by $\genDecompDispDTenSignedPct$ per cent. Figure~\ref{fig:decomp} weights households once; the 20-draw profile instead weights each household by its number of members (the basis of Figures~\ref{fig:decomp-ci}, \ref{fig:b7}, \ref{fig:b8} and~\ref{fig:b9}). Averaged over 20 displacement draws on that basis, the largest mean loss, \genDecompCiMaxLossPct~per cent, falls in decile~\genDecompCiMaxLossDecile, and the top decile changes by $\genDecompCiDTenSignedPct$ per cent (95 per cent interval for the mean $[\genDecompCiDTenLoPct, \genDecompCiDTenHiPct]$). The loss profile is therefore hump-shaped rather than rising with income: displacement losses concentrate in the upper-middle deciles, while capital-income gains offset them at the top. The bottom-decile result depends on how households are ranked. Under the HBAI ranking, decile~1 changes by $\genDecileRankHbaiDOneSignedPct$ per cent; ranked on income before pension contributions it changes by $\genDecileRankPrePensionDOneSignedPct$ per cent, and excluding the \genDecileRankExclHouseholds~households whose personal pension contributions exceed both \pounds\genDecileRankLargePpcFloorGbp{} and their earnings it changes by $\genDecileRankExclDOneSignedPct$ per cent. The bottom-decile change \genDecileOneRobustness: under HBAI, a small number of heavily weighted households with large personal pension contributions sit in decile~1 and receive capital income, so I do not report a bottom-decile result as a finding. % seed-0 components from results/jr16/fig4_4_decomposition.csv; 20-draw means from results/appendix/decomposition_ci.csv

\subsection{The shock-size grid}
\label{app:grid}

To move beyond the presets I simulate a grid of 66 scenarios, crossing eleven displacement rates (0 to 10 per cent of employees, allocated in proportion to exposure, as in the presets) with six wage uplifts (0 to 5 per cent), each combined with the capital income channel, all under exposure-proportional incidence. Figure~\ref{fig:griddisp} maps the change in average household disposable income. The corners bracket the range: with a 5 per cent wage gain and no job loss, average disposable income changes by $\genGridDispMaxPct$ per cent; with 10 per cent displacement and no wage gain it changes by $\genGridDispMinPct$ per cent. % results/jr16/grid.csv
The capital channel shifts the whole surface upwards: on its own (no displacement, no wage gain) it raises average disposable income by $\genGridCapOnlyDispPct$ per cent. With no wage growth, average disposable income still rises at 3 per cent displacement ($\genGridDispThreeZeroPct$ per cent) and first falls at 4 per cent ($\genGridDispFourZeroPct$ per cent); at the central 7 per cent displacement rate it falls with up to 4 per cent wage growth ($\genGridDispSevenFourPct$ per cent) and rises slightly with 5 per cent ($\genGridDispSevenFivePct$ per cent). One measurement issue deserves note: in PolicyEngine's UK aggregates, income tax plus National Insurance minus total benefit expenditure (which includes the state pension) is a negative number, so expressing revenue changes relative to that net position produces uninformative percentages. \subsection{Ripple-effect sensitivity}
\label{app:ripple}

The displacement scenarios of the main text hold non-displaced wages at
the uplifted baseline, so the ripple effects of
\citet{acemoglu2022tasks}---displaced workers bidding down the wages of
the non-displaced---are absent. Here I price that channel in reduced
form. After the central displacement draw, each displaced worker's
labour supply is routed across destination SOC2020 major groups using a
row-stochastic $9\times 9$ routing matrix with a zero diagonal: the
preferred source for this matrix, a pairwise retraining-cost matrix from
the \citet{hosseinilichtinger2026barriers} data release, does not exist
(the release contains only occupation-level measures, with no
origin--destination pairs), so I fall back on employment-proportional
routing, with each origin row proportional to destination employment
shares from the same ASHE 2025 employment weights used throughout the
paper. For each destination group I compute the proportional
labour-supply inflow $s_l$ (routed displaced FTE over baseline
employment), and every non-displaced employee in group $l$ takes a wage
cut of $\eta \times s_l$ of baseline earnings, composed additively with
the $+2.6$ per cent uplift. The parameter $\eta$ is an \emph{assumed
inverse-demand slope}---the proportional wage decline per unit
proportional labour-supply inflow---not a sourced own-wage elasticity,
and no conclusion about whether the central scenario is conservative
should be drawn from any single value of it; with 7 per cent of workers displaced the inflow
shares are around 7 per cent, so $\eta = 0.3$ implies wage cuts of
roughly 2 per cent across the non-displaced workforce. This is a
directional sensitivity, not an implementation of the
\citet{acemoglu2022tasks} propagation matrix: there is no task
structure, no equilibrium feedback, and the routing is exogenous.
Table~\ref{tab:ripple} reports the results for $\eta \in \{0, 0.15,
0.3, 0.5\}$.

Under this specification, the ripple channel loads mainly on the Exchequer. At $\eta =
0.3$ the fiscal cost rises by \pounds \genRippleCostRiseBn~billion, from \pounds \genCentralCostBn{} to
\pounds \genCentralRippleCostBn~billion, because thin, widespread wage cuts across the
non-displaced workforce forgo income tax and National Insurance at the
margin. The distributional estimates move less: BHC poverty rises by
\genRipplePovExtraPp~points more than in the central run (Table~\ref{tab:ripple} gives the Gini change); the cuts are imposed on employed workers concentrated in
the middle and upper deciles. Adding this particular ripple raises the
simulated fiscal cost; other routing and wage-response assumptions could
produce different results.

\subsection{Age and gender}
\label{app:agegender}

The incidence axis has a within-family age structure. Under exposure-proportional incidence, workers aged 16--24 account for \genAgeCentralYoungSharePct~per cent of the displaced in the central scenario (seed 0), because young people in the UK are concentrated in low-exposure service occupations; the largest displaced group is aged \genAgeCentralLargestBand, with \genAgeCentralLargestSharePct~per cent. The junior-concentrated family of Table~\ref{tab:incidence} reweights displacement probabilities towards junior workers while holding the aggregate displacement rate at 7 per cent. Because juniors are concentrated in occupation groups whose fixed quotas the tilt does not change, the shift is partial: the 16--24 share rises from \genAgeCentralYoungSharePct~to \genAgeYouthYoungSharePct~per cent and the 55--64 share moves from \genAgeCentralOlderSharePct~to \genAgeYouthOlderSharePct~per cent (seed 0), while the largest displaced group remains those aged \genAgeYouthLargestBand~(\genAgeYouthLargestSharePct~per cent). Over the 50 assignment draws the cost is \pounds \genIncJuniorCostMeanBn~$\pm$~\genIncJuniorCostSdBn~billion under the junior tilt against \pounds \genIncExposureCostMeanBn~$\pm$~\genIncExposureCostSdBn~billion under exposure-proportional incidence. The junior tilt is therefore an additional scenario assumption rather than a consequence of occupational exposure alone. % age shares: results/central.json, results/central_youth_tilted.json (seed 0)

If AI adoption in practice falls hardest on juniors through recruitment rather than dismissal---as the seniority-biased hiring evidence of \citet{hosseini2026} and the early-career employment declines in AI-exposed occupations documented by \citet{brynjolfsson2025} suggest---the fiscal consequences could differ materially from the broad-based scenarios above, because junior workers contribute little income tax and National Insurance. That channel, however, operates through hiring flows that stock-based simulation frameworks such as mine do not capture by construction.

Exposure also carries a gender gradient. Employed women have a mean C-AIOE of \genGenderFemaleExposure{} against \genGenderMaleExposure{} for employed men, reflecting women's concentration in administrative, professional and associate-professional work. In the central scenario this translates into a displacement rate of \genGenderFemaleRatePct~per cent for employed women against \genGenderMaleRatePct~per cent for men (means over 20 draws): women hold \genGenderFemaleEmpSharePct~per cent of employment and account for \genGenderFemaleDispSharePct~per cent of the displaced. % results/appendix/gender_incidence.json
The individual-level asymmetry echoes international evidence that female employment is more exposed to generative AI than male employment \citep{ilo2025}.

\subsection{Benefit caseloads}
\label{app:caseloads}

The fiscal aggregates above can also be read on the delivery side, as caseloads. In the central scenario (seed 0) the shock draws \genCaseloadNewUcBenunitsK,000 benefit units into Universal Credit while \genCaseloadExitUcBenunitsK,000 exit, a net caseload increase of \genCaseloadNetNewUcBenunitsK,000 benefit units, and annual UC spending rises by \pounds \genCaseloadUcSpendChangeBn~billion from a baseline of \pounds \genCaseloadUcBaselineBn~billion, of which \pounds \genCaseloadHousingBn~billion is the housing element within paid awards. The high scenario adds \genCaseloadHighNetK,000 net new benefit units and \pounds \genCaseloadHighSpendBn~billion, and the low scenario \genCaseloadLowNetK,000 benefit units and \pounds \genCaseloadLowSpendBn~billion. Caseload responses vary with incidence: the junior-concentrated family adds \genCaseloadJuniorNetK,000 net benefit units at a cost of \pounds \genCaseloadJuniorSpendBn~billion. Two accounting notes apply: only the benefit-unit figures are net of exits---the corresponding household and person counts (\genCaseloadNewUcHouseholdsK,000 and \genCaseloadNewUcPersonsK,000 in the central scenario) are gross new entrants---and the housing element is measured within paid awards, slightly overstating housing-specific cash. % results/caseloads/summary.csv

Because the capital channel scales dividends proportionally, its fiscal
size depends on how much dividend income the data carry. Restricted to
people with total income of at least \pounds 12{,}570, the population the
SPI covers, Microcosm's 2026 dividend income is \pounds \genDivTaxpayerBn~billion,
which is \genDivRatioAbsPct~per cent \genDivRatioDirection{} the HMRC SPI projection for
the same year (\pounds \genDivSpiBn~billion: the SPI 2023--24 outturn
projected to 2026 by PolicyEngine's data pipeline, summed over all income
bands from \pounds 12{,}570).\footnote{The projection is
\texttt{incomes\_projection.csv} in the policyengine-uk-data repository
(commit fa4ebe9), the source of Microcosm's own SPI calibration targets.
Microcosm matches the SPI targets band by band up to \pounds 1 million of
total income; in the untargeted band above it Microcosm holds
\pounds \genDivTopBandMicrocosmBn~billion of dividends against
\pounds \genDivTopBandSpiBn~billion in the projection.} I run three scaled
variants, each over \genDivDraws{} draws paired with the unscaled central
run, scaling the variable in both the baseline and the shocked runs and
only for people above the threshold:
\begin{itemize}
\item dividends scaled by one factor (\genDivScaleFactor) to the SPI total:
the central cost changes from \pounds \genDivCentralCostMeanBn~billion to
\pounds \genCovDivUniformCostMeanBn~billion (a paired increase of
\pounds\genCovDivUniformDiffCostAbsBn~billion), and the Gini change by
$\genCovDivUniformDiffGiniSignedPp$ points;
\item dividends scaled band by band to the SPI band totals (top-band factor
\genDivTopBandFactor): the cost is \pounds \genCovDivBandedCostMeanBn~billion
(a paired increase of \pounds\genCovDivBandedDiffCostAbsBn~billion);
\item savings interest scaled by \genSavScaleFactor{} to the SPI total of
\pounds \genSavSpiBn~billion: the cost is \pounds \genCovSavUniformCostMeanBn~billion
(a paired increase of \pounds\genCovSavUniformDiffCostAbsBn~billion).
\end{itemize}
I report these as sensitivities, not as alternative headlines: these data
do not show whether the gap reflects top-income dividends the projection
misses or calibration error. The Monte Carlo spread does not capture this
uncertainty, because the capital uplift is the same in every draw.
