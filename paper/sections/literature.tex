\section{Related literature}
\label{sec:literature}

\paragraph{Exposure and incidence.} Occupation-level exposure measures link AI capabilities to the abilities or tasks of each occupation \citep{webb2019, felten2021, tolan2021, eloundou2023, gmyrek2023, ilo2025}; UK applications include \citet{dsit2023} and the task-based index of \citet{henseke2025}. The government's January 2026 assessment reports, from IMF estimates, that around 70 per cent of UK workers are in exposed jobs and about half of those in high-complementarity roles, and it gives no displacement estimate \citep{govuk2026}. Exposure does not settle incidence. The complementarity adjustment of \citet{pizzinelli2023} and \citet{cazzaniga2024} separates exposure that substitutes for workers from exposure that augments them, and in a calibrated task model \citet{rockall2025} find that AI can reduce wage inequality by displacing high-income workers while raising wealth inequality through higher capital returns. The expertise-compression view holds that AI lowers the premia of experienced workers by letting less experienced ones perform their tasks \citep{autor2024, hosseinilichtinger2026barriers}, consistent with experimental gains concentrated among less experienced workers \citep{noy2023, brynjolfsson2025qje}. Early UK and US evidence points instead to junior workers: \citet{kleinteeselink2025} reports larger employment falls for junior positions at more exposed UK firms, \citet{hosseini2026} find junior employment falling after firm adoption in US r\'esum\'e data, and in their latest version \citet{brynjolfsson2025} report that employment of 22--25-year-olds in the most exposed US jobs stands 19 per cent below the path of less exposed peers, a pattern they describe as descriptive rather than causal. Danish administrative data rule out effects on earnings or recorded hours larger than 2 per cent two years after ChatGPT's launch \citep{humlum2025}. Because these views imply different incidence, I vary incidence rather than assume it.

\paragraph{Labour-market shocks in tax-benefit models.} \citet{dolls2012} define the income stabilisation coefficient, the share of a market-income shock absorbed by taxes and transfers, and show that it is larger for an unemployment shock than for a proportional income shock in Europe. \citet{brewer2021} run the Covid-19 employment shock and policy response through UKMOD. \citet{doorley2023automation} find that European tax-benefit systems muted the pass-through of routine-biased automation to household inequality, and \citet{doorley2026} run the 7 per cent displacement convention through the Irish SWITCH model, comparing exposure-proportional and uniform allocation. Evidence on the persistence of UK earnings losses after job loss \citep{upward2019} bears on the duration assumption. I add the UK, a fixed-quota comparison of five allocation rules with paired draws, and a comparison of job loss with wage cuts at the same gross earnings loss, which is the extensive-versus-intensive contrast of \citet{dolls2012} applied to an AI shock.

\paragraph{The public finance of AI.} If AI shifts income from labour to capital, labour-tax bases erode \citep{korinek2026}. The OBR models this in a technological-displacement scenario in its March 2026 outlook \citep{obr2026} and in a long-term scenario in which the wage share halves \citep{obr2026frs}; one US exercise runs alternative AI futures through the federal tax system without benefits \citep{iselin2026}. \citet{brollo2024} discuss unemployment insurance and capital taxation as fiscal responses to generative AI. A normative literature asks whether automation should be taxed \citep{guerreiro2022, costinot2023, thuemmel2023}, documents that the US tax code taxes labour more heavily than equipment \citep{acemoglu2020bpea}, and shows that slow reallocation and borrowing constraints can make automation inefficient \citep{beraja2025}; \citet{bastani2024} review capital taxation in this setting. This paper is positive rather than normative. It supplies the household tax-benefit layer under the OBR's aggregate scenarios: who loses what, how much the Exchequer absorbs, and how that depends on incidence and margin.
