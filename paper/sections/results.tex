\section{Results}
\label{sec:results}

I first report the central scenario, how the tax-benefit system absorbs it and where in the income distribution the loss falls, then the two comparisons that organise the paper: who loses the job, and how the earnings loss arrives. The section closes with the assumptions that move the central cost, an account of where the displaced wage bill goes, and a benchmark against the OBR.

\subsection{The central scenario and its three channels}
\label{sec:results-channels}

Table~\ref{tab:presets} summarises the central scenario and the two presets. Over 50 assignment draws, the central shock displaces \genCentralMcDisplacedMeanM~million employees, costs the Exchequer \pounds\genCentralMcCostMeanBn~billion a year (draw standard deviation \pounds\genCentralMcCostSdBn~billion; standard error of the mean \pounds\genCentralMcCostSeBn~billion), and raises the Gini coefficient by \genCentralMcGiniMeanPp~points from a baseline of \genBaselineGini. The exposure-proportional rule concentrates job loss in higher-income households: \genTransDOnePct~per cent of people in the bottom decile live through a transition to unemployment, against \genTransDTenPct~per cent in the top (Figure~\ref{fig:transition}). That gradient restates the allocation assumption; it is not evidence about who AI will displace.

The central cost nets three large flows. Running each channel alone on 20 paired draws, displacement costs \pounds\genChannelMcDispCostAbsBn~billion, while the survivor-wage and capital channels each raise net receipts by \pounds\genChannelMcWageCostAbsBn~billion; the channels sum to within \pounds\genChannelMcInteractionCostAbsBn~billion of the full shock. Per displaced worker, the displacement channel costs \pounds\genCostPerDisplacedGbp{} (mean of the per-draw ratio over \genCostPerDisplacedDraws~draws, standard deviation \pounds\genCostPerDisplacedSdGbp). By tax and benefit, the 50-draw cost combines lost income tax on earnings, net of the tax on survivors' wage gains (\pounds\genCostOtherIncomeTaxAbsBn~billion), employer NI (\pounds\genCostEmployerNiAbsBn~billion), employee NI (\pounds\genCostEmployeeNiAbsBn~billion) and higher Universal Credit (UC) spending (\pounds\genCostUcAbsBn~billion), against higher dividend tax (\pounds\genCostDividendTaxAbsBn~billion), higher savings tax (\pounds\genCostSavingsTaxAbsBn~billion) and lower childcare support (\pounds\genCostChildcareAbsBn~billion). The Exchequer loses the employer contributions on displaced workers' earnings whatever their incidence; whether the saved labour cost returns as taxable profit is the closure question of Section~\ref{sec:results-closure}. The government balance also omits the public sector's side of displacement: displaced public-sector employees cost \pounds\genCentralPaybillMeanBn~billion a year in pay, employer NI and employer pension contributions (mean over \genCentralPaybillDraws~draws, standard deviation \pounds\genCentralPaybillSdBn~billion; Table~\ref{tab:presets}). Whether that sum is a saving depends on whether the services they provided continue, which the simulation does not model.

The Gini rises because displaced workers lose all earnings, survivors gain, and the capital uplift accrues mainly in the top decile, which holds \genCapTopSharePct~per cent of baseline capital income. The rise does not depend on the capital channel: on its own the displacement channel raises the Gini by \genChannelMcDispGiniAbsPp~points (20 draws), and with the capital shock switched off the Gini rises in all \genAppGridNoCapGiniPositive{} shocked cells of the grid (Online Appendix~\ref{app:grid}).

\paragraph{Poverty.} Absolute BHC poverty rises by \genCentralMcPovBhcMeanPp~points (standard error \genCentralMcPovBhcSePp). I read this as an upper bound. Every displaced worker loses a full year of earnings against a poverty line frozen at its baseline value. On the relative line, which moves with the shocked median, the central rise is \genCentralMcRelPovBhcMeanPp~points.

\paragraph{A historical benchmark.} The framework over-predicts the poverty response to a past downturn. A backcast displaces 2.6 per cent of employees uniformly, matching the rise in the LFS unemployment rate over 2008--2010, with no wage or capital channel (means over five draws). It raises simulated absolute BHC poverty by $\genBackcastPovBhcPp$ points, absolute AHC poverty by $\genBackcastPovAhcPp$ points and the Gini by $\genBackcastGiniPp$ points, and changes relative BHC poverty by $\genBackcastRelPovPp$ points. Recorded HBAI outturns over 2007/08--2010/11 show absolute BHC poverty and the Gini roughly flat to slightly falling and relative poverty falling: benefits were uprated with high inflation while median incomes fell, and unemployment spells were of mixed length. The gap measures what the channels outside the simulation (uprating, duration, behavioural and macro-policy responses) absorbed in practice. Per point of uniform displacement the backcast gives $\genBackcastPerPointPp$ points of BHC poverty, against $\genUniformPerPointPp$ for the uniform 7 per cent family, which also carries the wage and capital channels.
% results/robustness/recession_backcast.json At seed~0, \genPovInDisplacedHhPct~per cent of people entering absolute poverty live with a displaced worker, and \genPovSoleEarnerPct~per cent of those households had no other earner.

\paragraph{What a displaced worker can claim.} A displaced employee meets the UK system through two routes. New-style Jobseeker's Allowance, a contributory benefit, pays \pounds\genJsaContribWeekGbp{} a week to a claimant aged 25 or over for up to 26 weeks; the simulation pays it only where the survey reports an award, so newly displaced workers do not receive it (Section~\ref{sec:limitations}). Universal Credit (UC) is means-tested on the benefit unit. In 2026--27 its standard allowance is \pounds\genUcSingleOldMonthGbp{} a month for a single claimant aged 25 or over and \pounds\genUcCoupleOldMonthGbp{} for a couple with one partner aged 25 or over, plus elements for children, housing, disability and childcare. A partner's earnings above the work allowance (\pounds\genUcWorkAllowanceHousingGbp{} a month with housing support and \pounds\genUcWorkAllowanceNoHousingGbp{} without, for claimants with children or limited capability for work) reduce the award by \genUcTaperPct~pence in the pound, and a benefit unit with capital above \pounds\genUcCapitalLimitGbp{} has no entitlement. Each benefit unit keeps the UC take-up decision drawn for it in the baseline data. A displaced worker with a working partner or savings above the limit therefore loses earnings with no UC response, while a displaced sole earner with no other income and savings below the limit receives the full standard allowance and, if renting, the housing element.

\paragraph{Caseloads.} In the seed-0 central draw the shock draws \genCaseloadNewUcBenunitsK,000 benefit units into UC while \genCaseloadExitUcBenunitsK,000 exit, a net increase of \genCaseloadNetNewUcBenunitsK,000, against \genCentralDisplacedTwoM~million displaced workers. Annual UC spending rises by \pounds\genCaseloadUcSpendChangeBn~billion from a baseline of \pounds\genCaseloadUcBaselineBn~billion, of which \pounds\genCaseloadHousingBn~billion is the housing element within paid awards. The high preset adds \genCaseloadHighNetK,000 net benefit units and \pounds\genCaseloadHighSpendBn~billion, the low preset \genCaseloadLowNetK,000 and \pounds\genCaseloadLowSpendBn~billion, and the junior-concentrated rule \genCaseloadJuniorNetK,000 and \pounds\genCaseloadJuniorSpendBn~billion. Only the benefit-unit figures are net of exits; the corresponding counts of households (\genCaseloadNewUcHouseholdsK,000) and persons (\genCaseloadNewUcPersonsK,000) are gross new entrants. % results/caseloads/summary.csv

\subsection{Where the income loss falls}
\label{sec:results-deciles}

Figure~\ref{fig:decomp-ci} reports the central-scenario change in disposable income by baseline decile, averaged over 20 displacement draws, with each household weighted by its number of members. The profile is hump-shaped: the largest mean loss, \genDecompCiMaxLossPct~per cent, falls in decile~\genDecompCiMaxLossDecile, because displacement losses concentrate in the upper-middle deciles while capital-income gains offset them at the top, where the change is $\genDecompCiDTenSignedPct$ per cent (95 per cent interval for the mean $[\genDecompCiDTenLoPct, \genDecompCiDTenHiPct]$). The bottom-decile result depends on how households are ranked. Under the HBAI ranking decile~1 changes by $\genDecileRankHbaiDOneSignedPct$ per cent; ranked on income before pension contributions it changes by $\genDecileRankPrePensionDOneSignedPct$ per cent, and excluding the \genDecileRankExclHouseholds~households whose personal pension contributions exceed both \pounds\genDecileRankLargePpcFloorGbp{} and their earnings it changes by $\genDecileRankExclDOneSignedPct$ per cent. A small number of heavily weighted households with large personal pension contributions sit in decile~1 under HBAI and receive capital income, so the bottom-decile change \genDecileOneRobustness, and I do not report it as a finding.

\citet{dolls2012} report that taxes and transfers absorb 47 per cent of an unemployment shock in the EU and 34 per cent in the US. The household-basis share here, \genObrStabTbAllPct~per cent, is measured on the same concept but with two differences in what the shock triggers. Their unemployment shock pays unemployment insurance where entitlement exists, whereas the simulation pays no new-style JSA to newly displaced workers. And their shock follows the characteristics of the existing unemployed, whereas exposure-proportional incidence places most of the loss in the upper half of the distribution, where means-tested benefits respond least.

\subsection{Who loses the job}
\label{sec:results-incidence}

Table~\ref{tab:incidence} reallocates the same expected number of displaced workers across the five rules of Section~\ref{sec:incidence}; survivor wages and capital income are identical at the person level. Across the four stylised rules the Exchequer cost runs from \pounds\genIncUniformCostMeanBn~billion (uniform) to \pounds\genIncCompressionCostMeanBn~billion (expertise compression); the top-loaded stress test costs \pounds\genIncKleinCostMeanBn~billion. The cost tracks the earnings of those displaced. Adding the survivor-wage and capital channels back, the displacement channel costs about \pounds\genFamPerWorkerUniformGbp{} per displaced worker under uniform incidence, \pounds\genFamPerWorkerExposureGbp{} under exposure-proportional, \pounds\genFamPerWorkerCompressionGbp{} under expertise compression and \pounds\genFamPerWorkerKleinGbp{} under the stress test (50-draw means, assuming the channels add up, as they do to within \pounds\genChannelMcInteractionCostAbsBn~billion in the central case).

Because the rules share assignment seeds, I compare them draw by draw. Uniform incidence costs \pounds\genPairUniExpCostMeanAbs~billion less than exposure-proportional incidence (paired 95 per cent interval $[\genPairUniExpCostLo, \genPairUniExpCostHi]$) and expertise compression \pounds\genPairCompExpCostMeanAbs~billion more ($[\genPairCompExpCostLo, \genPairCompExpCostHi]$); the junior tilt changes the cost by $\genPairJunExpCostMean$ billion ($[\genPairJunExpCostLo, \genPairJunExpCostHi]$), because the tilt operates within fixed occupation quotas and young workers sit mostly in low-exposure groups. These intervals describe assignment noise across 50 draws and narrow as draws are added; they carry no information about survey sampling or about which rule is right.

Poverty does not follow cost. Relative to exposure-proportional incidence, uniform incidence raises BHC poverty by a further $\genPairUniExpPovMean$ points ($[\genPairUniExpPovLo, \genPairUniExpPovHi]$) and the Gini by $\genPairUniExpGiniMean$ points while costing less, and expertise compression raises poverty by $\genPairCompExpPovMean$ points more ($[\genPairCompExpPovLo, \genPairCompExpPovHi]$) while costing more. Two routes could produce this ordering. Uniform incidence displaces more workers in lower-income households, which start closer to the line; the high-earner tilts displace more sole earners, whose households fall the full distance. The saved draws do not separate the two routes.

Figure~\ref{fig:incidence} shows the decile pattern behind these differences. Under every rule the share of each decile moving to unemployment rises with income, because employees concentrate in the higher deciles. Uniform incidence moves a larger share of the lower and middle deciles to unemployment than exposure-proportional incidence, and the top-loaded stress test a larger share of the top decile. The right panel places each rule's cost against its poverty change on the same single draw.

\paragraph{Age and gender.} Young workers are concentrated in low-exposure occupations: \genAgeYoungLowTertilePct~per cent of employees aged 16--24 work in the lowest exposure tertile. The junior tilt therefore operates within occupation quotas that place few losses on the young, and over 50 draws it changes the cost little (\pounds\genIncJuniorCostMeanBn~$\pm$~\genIncJuniorCostSdBn~billion, against \pounds\genIncExposureCostMeanBn~$\pm$~\genIncExposureCostSdBn~billion under exposure-proportional incidence). If AI reaches juniors mainly through reduced hiring \citep{hosseini2026, brynjolfsson2025}, the stock-based framework does not capture it. Exposure also carries a gender gradient. Employed women have a mean C-AIOE of \genGenderFemaleExposure{} against \genGenderMaleExposure{} for employed men, reflecting women's concentration in administrative, professional and associate professional work. Over 20 central draws the displacement rate is \genGenderFemaleRatePct~per cent for employed women and \genGenderMaleRatePct~per cent for men, so women, who hold \genGenderFemaleEmpSharePct~per cent of employment, account for \genGenderFemaleDispSharePct~per cent of the displaced, in line with international evidence that female employment is more exposed to generative AI \citep{ilo2025}.

Other assumptions move the cost less or in one direction. A flat 70 per cent UC take-up probability, against Microcosm's person-weighted baseline claim propensity of \genTakeupBaselinePropensityPct~per cent, raises the cost from \pounds\genDurCentralCostMeanBn~billion to \pounds\genTakeupCostMeanBn~billion (20 draws), and a reduced-form ripple effect in which displaced workers lower other workers' wages with an assumed slope of 0.3 raises it to \pounds\genCentralRippleCostBn~billion (seed~0; Online Appendix~\ref{app:ripple}). The low and high presets of Table~\ref{tab:presets} keep the full capital uplift while scaling displacement, which is why the low preset shows a net gain.

\paragraph{Dividend coverage.} Because the capital channel scales dividends proportionally, its fiscal size depends on how much dividend income the data carry. Restricted to people with total income of at least \pounds 12{,}570, the population the HMRC Survey of Personal Incomes covers, Microcosm's 2026 dividend income is \pounds\genDivTaxpayerBn~billion, \genDivRatioAbsPct~per cent \genDivRatioDirection{} the SPI projection for the same year (\pounds\genDivSpiBn~billion: the SPI 2023--24 outturn projected to 2026 by PolicyEngine's data pipeline).\footnote{The projection is \texttt{incomes\_projection.csv} in the policyengine-uk-data repository (commit fa4ebe9), the source of Microcosm's own SPI calibration targets. Microcosm matches the SPI targets band by band up to \pounds 1 million of total income; in the untargeted band above it Microcosm holds \pounds\genDivTopBandMicrocosmBn~billion of dividends against \pounds\genDivTopBandSpiBn~billion in the projection.} Three scaled variants, each over \genDivDraws{} draws paired with the unscaled central run, scale the variable in both the baseline and the shocked runs for people above the threshold. Scaling dividends by one factor (\genDivScaleFactor) to the SPI total raises the central cost from \pounds\genDivCentralCostMeanBn~billion to \pounds\genCovDivUniformCostMeanBn~billion (a paired increase of \pounds\genCovDivUniformDiffCostAbsBn~billion) and changes the Gini response by $\genCovDivUniformDiffGiniSignedPp$ points; scaling dividends band by band to the SPI band totals (top-band factor \genDivTopBandFactor) gives \pounds\genCovDivBandedCostMeanBn~billion (an increase of \pounds\genCovDivBandedDiffCostAbsBn~billion); and scaling savings interest by \genSavScaleFactor{} to the SPI total of \pounds\genSavSpiBn~billion gives \pounds\genCovSavUniformCostMeanBn~billion (an increase of \pounds\genCovSavUniformDiffCostAbsBn~billion). \subsection{A benchmark against the OBR}
\label{sec:results-obr}

The OBR's March 2026 outlook includes a technological-displacement scenario in which equilibrium unemployment rises to \genObrUnempScenPct~per cent, against \genObrUnempBasePct~per cent in the central forecast for 2030; higher productivity leaves GDP unchanged and real earnings do not rise, so the labour share falls and the profit share rises \citep[Box~2.2]{obr2026}. Borrowing is higher than in the central forecast by an average of \pounds\genObrBorrowingBn~billion a year: receipts are \pounds\genObrReceiptsLostBn~billion lower, from lower personal tax and VAT net of higher corporation tax, welfare spending is \pounds\genObrWelfareAddedBn~billion higher, and debt interest rises with the extra borrowing \citep[para.~6.18]{obr2026}.

I simulate the closest equivalent the household model allows. Displacement adds \genObrUnempRisePp~points of Microcosm's \genObrLabourForceM~million labour force to unemployment, which is \genObrRatePct~per cent of employees under exposure-proportional incidence, with no survivor-wage change and no capital shock (\genObrDraws~draws). Table~\ref{tab:obr} sets the simulated change in the government balance beside the OBR's lines. The simulation costs \pounds\genObrCostMeanBn~billion, about \pounds\genObrCostPerDisplacedGbp{} per displaced worker. Its receipts line, \pounds\genObrReceiptsBn~billion, exceeds the OBR's \pounds\genObrReceiptsLostBn~billion, and its welfare line, \pounds\genObrWelfareBn~billion, falls short of the OBR's \pounds\genObrWelfareAddedBn~billion.

The two sets of numbers measure different things in four ways. First, timing: the OBR figures average the forecast years, over which the unemployment gap builds up as the central forecast falls back to its equilibrium rate by 2030, whereas the simulation applies the full \genObrUnempRisePp-point rise in one year, so the OBR's average is smaller than its own effect once the full rise is in place. Second, coverage: the OBR's receipts include VAT on lower consumption and corporation tax on higher profits, and its borrowing includes debt interest; the simulation has neither consumption nor profit responses. Third, the OBR's welfare figure covers all welfare spending, including contributory benefits that the simulation does not pay to the newly displaced. Fourth, the OBR's scenario does not allocate unemployment across occupations, whereas exposure-proportional incidence displaces higher earners, whose income tax and NI make up most of the simulated cost. The simulated balance also excludes the public-sector pay bill of the displaced, \pounds\genObrLinePaybillBn~billion. The long-term counterpart in the OBR's fiscal risks report, in which the wage share of GDP halves by 2075--76, lowers tax receipts by nearly 4 percentage points of GDP because wages and salaries carry an average effective tax rate of 46 per cent against 27 per cent on profits \citep[Box~4.1]{obr2026frs}.
