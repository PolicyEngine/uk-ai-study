\section{Results}
\label{sec:results}

I first report the central scenario and how the tax-benefit system absorbs it, then the two comparisons that organise the paper: who loses the job, and how the earnings loss arrives. The section closes with the assumptions that move the central cost, an account of where the displaced wage bill goes, and a benchmark against the OBR.

\subsection{The central scenario and its three channels}
\label{sec:results-channels}

Table~\ref{tab:presets} summarises the central scenario and the two presets. Over 50 assignment draws, the central shock displaces \genCentralMcDisplacedMeanM~million employees, costs the Exchequer \pounds\genCentralMcCostMeanBn~billion a year (draw standard deviation \pounds\genCentralMcCostSdBn~billion; standard error of the mean \pounds\genCentralMcCostSeBn~billion), and raises the Gini coefficient by \genCentralMcGiniMeanPp~points from a baseline of \genBaselineGini. The exposure-proportional rule concentrates job loss in higher-income households: \genTransDOnePct~per cent of people in the bottom decile live through a transition to unemployment, against \genTransDTenPct~per cent in the top (Figure~\ref{fig:transition}). That gradient restates the allocation assumption; it is not evidence about who AI will displace.

The central cost nets three large flows. Running each channel alone on 20 paired draws, displacement costs \pounds\genChannelMcDispCostAbsBn~billion, while the survivor-wage and capital channels each raise net receipts by \pounds\genChannelMcWageCostAbsBn~billion; the channels sum to within \pounds\genChannelMcInteractionCostAbsBn~billion of the full shock. Per displaced worker, the displacement channel costs \pounds\genCostPerDisplacedGbp{} (mean of the per-draw ratio over \genCostPerDisplacedDraws~draws, standard deviation \pounds\genCostPerDisplacedSdGbp). By tax and benefit, the 50-draw cost combines lost income tax on earnings (\pounds\genCostOtherIncomeTaxAbsBn~billion), employer NI (\pounds\genCostEmployerNiAbsBn~billion), employee NI (\pounds\genCostEmployeeNiAbsBn~billion) and higher Universal Credit (UC) spending (\pounds\genCostUcAbsBn~billion), against higher dividend tax (\pounds\genCostDividendTaxAbsBn~billion), higher savings tax (\pounds\genCostSavingsTaxAbsBn~billion) and lower childcare support (\pounds\genCostChildcareAbsBn~billion). The Exchequer loses the employer contributions on displaced workers' earnings whatever their incidence; whether the saved labour cost returns as taxable profit is the closure question of Section~\ref{sec:results-closure}.

The Gini rises because displaced workers lose all earnings, survivors gain, and the capital uplift accrues mainly in the top decile, which holds \genCapTopSharePct~per cent of baseline capital income. The rise does not depend on the capital channel: on its own the displacement channel raises the Gini by \genChannelMcDispGiniAbsPp~points (20 draws), and with the capital shock switched off the Gini rises in all \genAppGridNoCapGiniPositive{} shocked cells of the grid (Online Appendix~\ref{app:grid}).

\paragraph{Poverty.} Absolute BHC poverty rises by \genCentralMcPovBhcMeanPp~points (standard error \genCentralMcPovBhcSePp). I read this as an upper bound. Every displaced worker loses a full year of earnings against a poverty line frozen at its baseline value, and the framework over-predicts the poverty response to a past downturn: a uniform 2.6 per cent displacement sized to the 2008--09 rise in unemployment raises simulated absolute BHC poverty by \genBackcastPovBhcAbsPp~points (five draws), while recorded HBAI absolute poverty was roughly flat over 2007/08--2010/11, when benefits were uprated with high inflation and spells were of mixed length (Appendix~\ref{app:backcast}). On the relative line, which moves with the shocked median, the central rise is \genCentralMcRelPovBhcMeanPp~points. At seed~0, \genPovInDisplacedHhPct~per cent of people entering absolute poverty live with a displaced worker, and \genPovSoleEarnerPct~per cent of those households had no other earner.

\subsection{Who loses the job}
\label{sec:results-incidence}

Table~\ref{tab:incidence} reallocates the same expected number of displaced workers across the five rules of Section~\ref{sec:incidence}; survivor wages and capital income are identical at the person level. Across the four stylised rules the Exchequer cost runs from \pounds\genIncUniformCostMeanBn~billion (uniform) to \pounds\genIncCompressionCostMeanBn~billion (expertise compression); the top-loaded stress test costs \pounds\genIncKleinCostMeanBn~billion. The cost tracks the earnings of those displaced. Adding the survivor-wage and capital channels back, the displacement channel costs about \pounds\genFamPerWorkerUniformGbp{} per displaced worker under uniform incidence, \pounds\genFamPerWorkerExposureGbp{} under exposure-proportional, \pounds\genFamPerWorkerCompressionGbp{} under expertise compression and \pounds\genFamPerWorkerKleinGbp{} under the stress test (50-draw means, assuming the channels add up, as they do to within \pounds\genChannelMcInteractionCostAbsBn~billion in the central case).

Because the rules share assignment seeds, I compare them draw by draw. Uniform incidence costs \pounds\genPairUniExpCostMeanAbs~billion less than exposure-proportional incidence (paired 95 per cent interval $[\genPairUniExpCostLo, \genPairUniExpCostHi]$) and expertise compression \pounds\genPairCompExpCostMeanAbs~billion more ($[\genPairCompExpCostLo, \genPairCompExpCostHi]$); the junior tilt changes the cost by $\genPairJunExpCostMean$ billion ($[\genPairJunExpCostLo, \genPairJunExpCostHi]$), because the tilt operates within fixed occupation quotas and young workers sit mostly in low-exposure groups. These intervals describe assignment noise across 50 draws and narrow as draws are added; they carry no information about survey sampling or about which rule is right.

Poverty does not follow cost. Relative to exposure-proportional incidence, uniform incidence raises BHC poverty by a further $\genPairUniExpPovMean$ points ($[\genPairUniExpPovLo, \genPairUniExpPovHi]$) and the Gini by $\genPairUniExpGiniMean$ points while costing less, and expertise compression raises poverty by $\genPairCompExpPovMean$ points more ($[\genPairCompExpPovLo, \genPairCompExpPovHi]$) while costing more. Two routes could produce this ordering. Uniform incidence displaces more workers in lower-income households, which start closer to the line; the high-earner tilts displace more sole earners, whose households fall the full distance. The saved draws do not separate the two routes; the runs specified for the next revision record the decile flows that would (Section~\ref{sec:limitations}).

The margin changes how much of each lost pound the Exchequer absorbs. Displacement alone (seed~0, no wage or capital channel) removes \pounds\genFaWageBillBn~billion of earnings and costs the government balance \genAbsJobGovPct~pence per pound; on the household basis that excludes employer NI (income tax, employee NI and benefits, the stabilisation coefficient of \citet{dolls2012}) it costs \genAbsJobHhPct~pence. Cutting survivors' wages from the survivor-wage grid's 0 to $-2.6$ per cent row costs \genAbsWageGovPct~pence per pound on the government balance. A wage cut removes pounds taxed at the worker's marginal rates of income tax and NI, and withdraws UC at the taper; job loss removes whole earnings, including the slice covered by the personal allowance and NI thresholds, and the UC it adds is small relative to the tax it forgoes. Poverty moves the other way: concentrated losses push displaced households across the line, while the same loss spread across \genDataEmployeesM~million employees moves few households below it.

Other assumptions move the cost less or in one direction. A flat 70 per cent UC take-up probability, against Microcosm's person-weighted baseline claim propensity of \genTakeupBaselinePropensityPct~per cent, raises the cost from \pounds\genDurCentralCostMeanBn~billion to \pounds\genTakeupCostMeanBn~billion (20 draws); scaling dividends to the HMRC SPI projection, which Microcosm exceeds by \genDivRatioAbsPct~per cent, raises it to \pounds\genDivSpiCostMeanBn~billion (20 draws; Online Appendix~\ref{app:dataset-change}); and a reduced-form ripple effect in which displaced workers lower other workers' wages with an assumed slope of 0.3 raises it to \pounds\genCentralRippleCostBn~billion (seed~0; Online Appendix~\ref{app:ripple}). The low and high presets of Table~\ref{tab:presets} keep the full capital uplift while scaling displacement, which is why the low preset shows a net gain.

\subsection{Where the displaced wage bill goes}
\label{sec:results-closure}

The central scenario imposes the capital uplift independently of the labour shock, and its government balance counts no corporation tax. Table~\ref{tab:closure} replaces the uplift with an explicit account of where the displaced wage bill goes, on the seed-0 draw where every flow is available. Displaced workers lose \pounds\genFaWageBillBn~billion of earnings and survivors gain \pounds\genFaSurvivorGainBn~billion, so labour income falls by \pounds\genFaNetLabourBn~billion. The central scenario instead adds \pounds\genFaCapUpliftBn~billion of household capital income (\pounds\genFaDivUpliftBn~billion of dividends and \pounds\genFaIntUpliftBn~billion of interest).

In the closed account a share $\phi$ of the net labour-income fall reappears as company profit, which pays corporation tax at 25 per cent; companies pay out \genFaPayoutPct~per cent of post-tax profit, and a share $s$ of that reaches UK households, the remainder going to overseas owners and pension funds, whose returns the household simulation does not tax. Household dividends pay tax at the \genFaDivTaxRatePct~per cent average rate the simulated capital channel implies. The interest uplift has no counterpart in this account. The closed balance keeps the displacement and survivor-wage channels and their interaction and replaces the capital channel with corporation tax and dividend tax on the implied payout.

Only when nearly all of the net labour-income fall becomes profit and the payout reaches UK households does the closed account return less than the central cost; at $\phi=0.5$ the cost roughly doubles. The central capital uplift adds more dividend income (\pounds\genFaDivUpliftBn~billion) than the closed account delivers to households even at $\phi=1$ and $s=1$ (\pounds\genFaHhDivPhiOneShareOneBn~billion), so the central scenario's sign rests on a capital uplift that the labour shock alone does not finance. This account supersedes the earlier labour-tax gap exercise, which priced corporation tax with the capital shock off and counted no dividends.

\subsection{A benchmark against the OBR}
\label{sec:results-obr}

The OBR's March 2026 outlook includes a technological-displacement scenario in which equilibrium unemployment rises to \genObrUnempScenPct~per cent against \genObrUnempBasePct~per cent in the central forecast for 2030, higher productivity leaves GDP unchanged, and real earnings do not rise, so the labour share falls and the profit share rises \citep[Box~2.2]{obr2026}. Borrowing is higher by an average of \pounds\genObrBorrowingBn~billion a year: receipts are \pounds\genObrReceiptsLostBn~billion lower, net of higher corporation tax, and welfare spending is \pounds\genObrWelfareAddedBn~billion higher \citep[para.~6.18]{obr2026}. The OBR scenario is closest to the closed account of Table~\ref{tab:closure} with $\phi$ near one and no survivor-wage gain, on a much smaller head count. At the displacement channel's cost of \pounds\genCostPerDisplacedGbp{} per displaced worker, \pounds\genObrBorrowingBn~billion corresponds to about \genObrImpliedDisplacedM~million displaced employees, against \genCentralMcDisplacedMeanM~million in the central case. The comparison is indicative: the OBR counts VAT and corporation tax, which this simulation holds fixed or omits, its welfare figure covers all benefits rather than the household-level entitlements simulated here, and its scenario does not allocate unemployment across occupations. Its long-term counterpart, in which the wage share of GDP halves by 2075--76, lowers tax receipts by nearly 4 percentage points of GDP because wages and salaries carry an average effective tax rate of 46 per cent against 27 per cent on profits \citep[Box~4.1]{obr2026frs}. A run specified for the next revision sizes displacement to the OBR's unemployment rise with flat earnings and no capital windfall and compares its receipts and welfare with the OBR's line by line.
