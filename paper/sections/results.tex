\section{Results}
\label{sec:results}

I first report the central scenario and its household mediation, then vary shock size, incidence and adjustment margin. Seed and draw conventions follow Section~\ref{sec:microsim}; $\pm$ values are standard deviations across assignment draws, not sampling standard errors.

\subsection{The three market-income channels}
\label{sec:results-channels}

The central scenario sets a 7 per cent displacement quota, allocated across occupations in proportion to exposure (at seed 0 it displaces \genCentralDisplacedTwoM~million workers, a realised rate of \genCentralRealisedRatePct~per cent), and grants every surviving employee a wage uplift proportional to occupational complementarity, averaging 2.6 per cent, alongside the capital income channel. Figure~\ref{fig:transition} plots the share of each equivalised disposable income decile that transitions from employment to unemployment. The gradient is \genTransMonotone: \genTransDOnePct~per cent of the bottom decile transitions, against \genTransDTenPct~per cent at the top. % results/jr16/fig4_1_transition_by_decile.csv
The gradient is not an empirical finding about who AI will in fact displace; it is the exposure-proportional allocation assumption made visible. Because the professional, associate-professional and administrative occupations that score highest on the C-AIOE index sit in the upper half of the income distribution, allocating displacement in proportion to occupational exposure necessarily concentrates job loss among higher-income households. \citet{doorley2026} obtain a similarly top-concentrated simulated profile for Ireland using different microdata and a finer occupational classification. Both results are conditional on exposure-proportional allocation and should not be read as evidence of realised incidence. Section~\ref{sec:results-incidence} therefore varies that assumption.

The wage channel, by contrast, is almost distributionally flat. Figure~\ref{fig:wages} shows the percentage change in employment income among workers who remain employed: gains run from \genWageGainDOnePct~per cent in the bottom decile to \genWageGainDTenPct~per cent in the top, % results/jr16/fig4_2_wage_gain_by_decile.csv
a spread of about 0.2 percentage points around the 2.6 per cent average uplift. Complementarity varies little across the deciles in which people work, so the uplift is close to uniform. Between the two labour-market channels, cross-decile variation comes from the displacement margin. That allocation is a property of the scenario design rather than a finding: in the US evidence of \citet{acemoglu2022tasks}, task displacement operates chiefly through relative wage declines, which account for 50--70 per cent of changes in the wage structure. Section~\ref{sec:results-wagemargin} examines this alternative: a wage-margin implementation of the same headline calibration shifts losses from benefit caseloads to earnings, with a higher fiscal cost and a smaller poverty and inequality response.

The third channel is capital income. The scenario assumes part of the productivity gain is capitalised into returns on existing capital, applying a uniform proportional uplift to interest and dividend income (Section~\ref{sec:shocks}). Although the percentage change is identical across deciles by construction, Figure~\ref{fig:capital} shows the incidence in pounds is heavily concentrated: the top decile holds \genCapTopSharePct~per cent of all capital income (\pounds \genCapTopBn~billion of the \pounds \genCapTotalBn~billion baseline aggregate), the top two deciles together \genCapTopTwoSharePct~per cent, and the bottom decile \genCapBottomSharePct~per cent. Under this proportional allocation, most simulated capital-income gains accrue to households already near the top of the distribution.

The capital channel also changes the fiscal result, because the uplift is proportional and the extra interest and dividends are taxed at the household level. Running each channel of the central shock on its own, with the other two held at baseline, over 20 paired draws, the displacement channel costs the Exchequer \pounds\genChannelMcDispCostAbsBn~billion, while the survivor-wage channel raises net receipts by \pounds\genChannelMcWageCostAbsBn~billion and the capital channel by \pounds\genChannelMcCapitalCostAbsBn~billion. The three single-channel results sum to a cost of \pounds\genChannelMcSumCostAbsBn~billion, against \pounds\genChannelMcCentralCostAbsBn~billion for the full shock on the same draws, an interaction of \pounds\genChannelMcInteractionCostAbsBn~billion. Adding back the capital channel's receipts to the full-shock cost gives \pounds\genChannelMcExclCapitalCostAbsBn~billion, the cost the other two channels imply on these draws. The headline cost is therefore a net figure of large offsetting flows, and it depends on the assumed capital-income uplift (Section~\ref{sec:shocks}) and on how much dividend income the microdata carry at the top. % results/robustness/dividend_coverage.json

By tax and benefit, the 50-draw central cost of \pounds\genCostTotalAbsBn~billion combines four losses to the Exchequer with three offsets. Employer National Insurance falls by \pounds\genCostEmployerNiAbsBn~billion, employee National Insurance by \pounds\genCostEmployeeNiAbsBn~billion and income tax other than on savings and dividends by \pounds\genCostOtherIncomeTaxAbsBn~billion, and Universal Credit spending rises by \pounds\genCostUcAbsBn~billion. Against these, dividend tax rises by \pounds\genCostDividendTaxAbsBn~billion and savings tax by \pounds\genCostSavingsTaxAbsBn~billion, so income tax as a whole rises by \pounds\genCostIncomeTaxAbsBn~billion; childcare support spending falls by \pounds\genCostChildcareAbsBn~billion; and the remaining items net to less than \pounds 0.1~billion. Employer contributions fall with each displaced employee's earnings. Excluding them, the central scenario is a net gain to the Exchequer of \pounds\genCostExclEmployerNiAbsBn~billion; this is the relevant figure for readers who treat employer contributions as part of labour cost rather than as a tax on households. % results/robustness/central_cost_decomposition.json

Microcosm is calibrated to HMRC Survey of Personal Incomes income by band up to \pounds 1 million of total income. Its 2026 dividend income of people with total income of at least \pounds 12{,}570 is \genDivRatioAbsPct~per cent \genDivRatioDirection{} the SPI projection for that year; scaling those dividends to the projection changes the central cost from \pounds \genDivCentralCostMeanBn~billion to \pounds \genDivSpiCostMeanBn~billion (means over \genDivDraws~shared draws; Appendix~\ref{app:dataset-change} reports banded and savings-interest variants). The processed survey file used in an earlier version of this paper carried almost no dividends, so its capital channel ran almost entirely through savings interest: it held \pounds 23 billion of baseline capital income, against \pounds \genBaseCapitalBn~billion here. % earlier file: results/jr16/fig4_3_capital_by_decile.csv at commit 5d75106

\subsection{Household mediation of the shock}
\label{sec:results-mediation}

The tax-benefit system converts each market-income loss into a smaller disposable-income loss; Figure~\ref{fig:decomp} decomposes that mediation by decile into market income, benefits, and taxes and contributions, all expressed relative to baseline disposable income. The decomposition is household-level and additive: market income change plus benefit change minus tax change equals the disposable income change up to an explicit residual, which collects perimeter items inside the HBAI concept (council tax and pension deductions) and never exceeds \genDecompResidualMaxPct~per cent of baseline income in any decile.

The capital channel reshapes this mediation. In the seed-0 draw the largest disposable-income loss falls in decile~\genDecompWorstDecile: market income changes by $\genDecompMarketWorstSignedPct$ per cent of baseline disposable income, benefits by $\genDecompBenWorstSignedPct$ per cent and lower taxes and contributions return $\genDecompTaxWorstSignedPct$ per cent, leaving, with the HBAI perimeter items, a disposable-income change of $\genDecompDispWorstSignedPct$ per cent. Benefits and lower taxes and contributions offset about \genDecompAbsorbedTbWorstPct~per cent of the market loss, and \genDecompAbsorbedWorstPct~per cent including the perimeter items. In the top decile the capital uplift offsets the earnings lost by displaced workers: market income changes by $\genDecompMarketDTenSignedPct$ per cent and disposable income by $\genDecompDispDTenSignedPct$ per cent. Figure~\ref{fig:decomp} weights households once; the 20-draw profile instead weights each household by its number of members (the basis of Figures~\ref{fig:decomp-ci}, \ref{fig:b7}, \ref{fig:b8} and~\ref{fig:b9}). Averaged over 20 displacement draws on that basis, the largest mean loss, \genDecompCiMaxLossPct~per cent, falls in decile~\genDecompCiMaxLossDecile, and the top decile changes by $\genDecompCiDTenSignedPct$ per cent (95 per cent interval for the mean $[\genDecompCiDTenLoPct, \genDecompCiDTenHiPct]$). The loss profile is therefore hump-shaped rather than rising with income: displacement losses concentrate in the upper-middle deciles, while capital-income gains offset them at the top. The bottom-decile result depends on how households are ranked. Under the HBAI ranking, decile~1 changes by $\genDecileRankHbaiDOneSignedPct$ per cent; ranked on income before pension contributions it changes by $\genDecileRankPrePensionDOneSignedPct$ per cent, and excluding the \genDecileRankExclHouseholds~households whose personal pension contributions exceed both \pounds\genDecileRankLargePpcFloorGbp{} and their earnings it changes by $\genDecileRankExclDOneSignedPct$ per cent. The bottom-decile change \genDecileOneRobustness: under HBAI, a small number of heavily weighted households with large personal pension contributions sit in decile~1 and receive capital income, so I do not report a bottom-decile result as a finding. % seed-0 components from results/jr16/fig4_4_decomposition.csv; 20-draw means from results/appendix/decomposition_ci.csv

\subsection{Fiscal and distributional aggregates}
\label{sec:results-aggregates}

Table~\ref{tab:presets} summarises the preset scenarios. In the central scenario (seed 0, realised displacement rate \genCentralRealisedRatePct~per cent) the annual Exchequer cost is \pounds \genCentralCostBn~billion: lost income tax and National Insurance on displaced workers and higher benefit spending, net of higher receipts from the survivor-wage and capital channels. Poverty rises by \genCentralPovBhcPp~percentage points before housing costs (\genCentralPovAhcPp~points after housing costs) and the Gini coefficient of equivalised disposable income rises by \genCentralGiniChangePp~percentage points from a baseline of \genBaselineGini. The low scenario---a 1 per cent displacement rate with no wage gain, which I treat as a sensitivity case rather than an estimate drawn from the literature---improves the Exchequer balance by \pounds \genLowGainBn~billion, because the revenue from the capital-income uplift exceeds the cost of 1 per cent displacement; poverty changes by $\genLowPovBhcSignedPp$ (BHC) and $\genLowPovAhcSignedPp$ (AHC) points and the Gini by $\genLowGiniSignedPp$ points. The high scenario, at 13 per cent displacement, costs \pounds \genHighCostBn~billion a year with poverty up \genHighPovBhcPp~points. PolicyEngine's before-housing-costs poverty line is an \emph{absolute} threshold, so these poverty changes are measured against a fixed threshold, not relative to a shock-shifted median. On the relative (60 per cent of contemporaneous median) BHC line the central scenario changes poverty by $\genCentralMcRelPovBhcMeanSignedPp \pm \genCentralMcRelPovBhcSdPp$ percentage points over 50 draws, against $\genCentralMcPovBhcMeanSignedPp$ points on the absolute line; the relative line moves with the shocked median. % results/robustness/central_monte_carlo.json relative_poverty_change_bhc_pp

The scenario anchors are calibration conventions rather than forecasts (Section~\ref{sec:scenarios}); in particular, the low scenario is not an employment estimate from \citet{acemoglu2025}, whose 0.9--1.1 per cent figure refers to ten-year GDP growth.

Absolute poverty barely moved in recent UK downturns, so I decompose the central poverty response. The \genPovNetPp-point net change (seed 0) is a gross inflow of \genPovInflowM~million people (\genPovInflowPp~points), \genPovInDisplacedHhPct~per cent of them in households containing a displaced worker, net of a \genPovOutflowM-million outflow (\genPovOutflowPp~points) lifted over the line by the capital and survivor-wage channels. The entrants come from across baseline deciles 2--10 (\genPovTopDecilePct~per cent from the top decile, where a displaced sole earner falls the full distance), their households start at a median \genPovEntrantRatio~times the poverty line, and they land at a median \genPovEntrantShortfallPct~per cent below it. \genPovSoleEarnerPct~per cent depended entirely on the displaced worker's earnings. The magnitude follows from the framework's polar assumptions---full-year earnings loss and Universal Credit replacement rates against a baseline-fixed line; the duration and wage-margin sensitivities below show how it changes when those assumptions are relaxed, and a 2008--09-sized backcast in Appendix~\ref{app:backcast} quantifies how much of a recession's measured poverty response the excluded uprating and duration channels absorb in practice. % results/robustness/poverty_decomposition.json

Seed 0 lies at the low end of the 50 exposure-proportional draws for poverty and inequality: its BHC poverty change ranks \genSeedZeroRankPovBhc{} of 50 from the bottom, its AHC change \genSeedZeroRankPovAhc{} and its Gini change \genSeedZeroRankGini{}, while its cost ranks \genSeedZeroRankCost. Single-draw (seed-0) tables therefore understate the mean poverty and Gini responses; their cost figures sit close to the mean. Across the 50 assignment draws, the mean Exchequer cost is \pounds \genCentralMcCostMeanBn~billion (assignment-draw standard deviation \pounds \genCentralMcCostSdBn~billion; range \pounds \genCentralMcCostMinBn--\genCentralMcCostMaxBn~billion), with mean poverty and Gini increases of $\genCentralMcPovBhcMeanPp \pm \genCentralMcPovBhcSdPp$ and $\genCentralMcGiniMeanPp \pm \genCentralMcGiniSdPp$ percentage points. Replacing C-AIOE with the DSIT AIOE mapping lowers the seed-0 Exchequer cost by \pounds\genIndexDsitDiffAbsBn~billion, and the GPT-exposure measure of \citet{eloundou2023} lowers it by \pounds\genIndexEloundouDiffAbsBn~billion. Appendix~\ref{app:index-sensitivity} reports the transition gradients under each index. % results/robustness/index_sensitivity_full.json

Two tested assumptions change the headline numbers. The first is displacement duration. The central scenario removes a full year of earnings from every displaced worker. Under a 50 per cent earnings-retention hybrid---annual earnings are halved while employment status and hours remain those of full-year unemployment, so this approximates rather than models a six-month spell---the central cost of \pounds \genDurCentralCostMeanBn~billion (the central scenario on the same \genDurTakeupDraws~draws) turns into a net gain to the Exchequer of \pounds \genDurSixMoCostMeanAbsBn~$\pm$~\genDurSixMoCostSdBn~billion, and the BHC poverty change falls to $\genDurSixMoPovBhcMeanSignedPp \pm \genDurSixMoPovBhcSdPp$ points, over \genDurTakeupDraws~draws. Halving the full-year cost on the same draws would give \pounds \genDurCentralCostHalfBn~billion. The sign changes because the hybrid shrinks only the displacement channel: workers who keep half a year's earnings remain taxpayers and draw less means-tested support, while the survivor-wage and capital channels, which raise net receipts by \pounds\genChannelMcWageCostAbsBn~billion (survivor wages) and \pounds\genChannelMcCapitalCostAbsBn~billion (capital income) in the channel split, do not depend on duration. Fiscal and poverty magnitudes in this paper scale with the expected out-of-work duration, and the headline figures describe a full-year no-reemployment case. The second is benefit take-up. Imposing a 70 per cent Universal Credit take-up probability (drawn identically in baseline and shocked worlds) raises the cost from \pounds \genDurCentralCostMeanBn~billion to \pounds \genTakeupCostMeanBn~$\pm$~\genTakeupCostSdBn~billion and lowers the poverty change to $\genTakeupPovBhcMeanSignedPp \pm \genTakeupPovBhcSdPp$ points. Microcosm's own take-up flags give a person-weighted baseline claim propensity of \genTakeupBaselinePropensityPct~per cent, so a flat 70 per cent raises claiming among newly entitled households: Universal Credit spending then rises by more, and fewer of them fall below the line. Microcosm's own take-up flags already apply in the central draws, where higher Universal Credit spending accounts for \pounds\genCostUcAbsBn~billion of the cost. % results/robustness/duration_takeup_sensitivity.json (variant half_earnings_retention)

\subsection{The shock-size grid}
\label{sec:results-grid}

To move beyond the presets I simulate a grid of 66 scenarios, crossing eleven displacement rates (0 to 10 per cent of employees, allocated in proportion to exposure, as in the presets) with six wage uplifts (0 to 5 per cent), each combined with the capital income channel, all under exposure-proportional incidence. Figure~\ref{fig:griddisp} maps the change in average household disposable income. The corners bracket the range: with a 5 per cent wage gain and no job loss, average disposable income changes by $\genGridDispMaxPct$ per cent; with 10 per cent displacement and no wage gain it changes by $\genGridDispMinPct$ per cent. % results/jr16/grid.csv
The capital channel shifts the whole surface upwards: on its own (no displacement, no wage gain) it raises average disposable income by $\genGridCapOnlyDispPct$ per cent. With no wage growth, average disposable income still rises at 3 per cent displacement ($\genGridDispThreeZeroPct$ per cent) and first falls at 4 per cent ($\genGridDispFourZeroPct$ per cent); at the central 7 per cent displacement rate it falls with up to 4 per cent wage growth ($\genGridDispSevenFourPct$ per cent) and rises slightly with 5 per cent ($\genGridDispSevenFivePct$ per cent). Whether AI raises or lowers average living standards in this grid therefore depends on the balance between displacement and wage growth, and on the assumed capital-return shock, all of which are uncertain.

Figure~\ref{fig:gridrev} shows the corresponding fiscal picture. One measurement issue deserves note: in PolicyEngine's UK aggregates, income tax plus National Insurance minus total benefit expenditure (which includes the state pension) is a negative number, so expressing revenue changes relative to that net position produces uninformative percentages. I therefore express the net revenue change relative to gross income tax and National Insurance receipts as simulated on the household microdata (about \pounds \genGridReceiptsBn~billion); this base is smaller than headline OBR receipts, which include employer contributions and income outside the household universe. Two consequences follow. First, the grid's revenue measure is income tax plus National Insurance minus cash benefit expenditure, a narrower concept than the full government-balance measure behind the \pounds \genCentralMcCostMeanBn~billion headline cost of Section~\ref{sec:results-aggregates}, which also includes employer National Insurance, indirect taxes and other levies. Over the 50 central draws the narrow cash basis shows a net gain of \pounds\genCostNarrowCashAbsBn~billion, against a cost of \pounds\genCostTotalAbsBn~billion on the government balance; the gap of \pounds\genCostBasisGapAbsBn~billion matches the employer National Insurance component of \pounds\genCostEmployerNiAbsBn~billion, and the two measures should not be read off the same surface. Second, on that narrower basis the grid spans a gain of \pounds \genGridRevGainBn~billion, or \genGridRevGainPct~per cent of receipts (5 per cent wage growth, no displacement), to a loss of \pounds \genGridRevLossBn~billion, or \genGridRevLossPct~per cent of receipts, in the worst cell (10 per cent displacement, no wage growth). On this narrow basis the capital shock alone adds \pounds \genGridCapOnlyRevBn~billion of net revenue. At the central displacement rate, net revenue falls by \pounds\genGridRevSevenZeroAbsBn~billion with no wage offset (\genGridRevSevenZeroAbsPct~per cent of receipts) and rises by \pounds\genGridRevSevenThreeAbsBn~billion with a 3 per cent wage uplift. % results/jr16/grid.csv

\subsection{The incidence axis: who bears the shock}
\label{sec:results-incidence}

Everything so far conditions on exposure-proportional incidence. But allocation is at least as uncertain as shock size, so I reallocate the same central calibration across five families. Four are stylised: exposure-proportional, junior-concentrated, expertise-compression (implemented as a high-earner displacement tilt, not the \citet{hosseinilichtinger2026barriers} wage mechanism; Section~\ref{sec:incidence}) and uniform. The fifth is a Klein-anchored top-loaded stress test: exposure-proportional draws multiplied by an author-imposed junior tilt (1.29) and wage-tier tilt (9.6 above, 0.6 below median person earnings). Table~\ref{tab:incidence} gives means and assignment-draw standard deviations over 50 paired seeds.

The two top-loaded families raise BHC poverty by more than exposure-proportional incidence ($\genIncCompressionPovBhcMeanSignedPp$ and $\genIncKleinPovBhcMeanSignedPp$ against $\genIncExposurePovBhcMeanSignedPp$ points), and uniform incidence raises both poverty and the Gini by more while costing less. Whether a displaced household falls below the fixed line depends on how far its income falls relative to the line and on its entitlement to means-tested support, and both differ across the families; I do not decompose these differences further. % paired intervals from results/robustness/incidence_draws_five.csv (50 shared seeds)

Two families are author-imposed top-loaded calibrations. The mean cost under expertise compression is \pounds \genIncCompressionCostMeanBn~billion and changes BHC poverty by $\genIncCompressionPovBhcMeanSignedPp$ points. The Klein-anchored stress test has means of \pounds \genIncKleinCostMeanBn~billion and $\genIncKleinPovBhcMeanSignedPp$ points; its AHC effect is evaluated over the same 50 assignment draws. Its wage-tier multiplier produces the most top-tilted seed-0 gradient, from \genIncKleinTransDOnePct~per cent transitioning in the bottom decile to \genIncKleinTransDTenPct~per cent in the top. These magnitudes are conditional on the stress-test mapping and are not estimates transported from Klein Teeselink.

The 50-draw mean Gini change across the five simulated families runs from $\genIncFamilyGiniMeanMinSignedPp$ to $\genIncFamilyGiniMeanMaxSignedPp$ percentage points, and the Gini rises in all 66 grid cells (in the appendix's capital-off variant of the grid the null cell with no shock at all is exactly zero, as it must be). The Klein-anchored minus expertise-compression Gini difference is $\genPairKleinCompGiniMean$ points ($[\genPairKleinCompGiniLo, \genPairKleinCompGiniHi]$, \genPairKleinCompGiniSig). The directional result is conditional on these displacement calibrations.

\subsection{The adjustment margin: wage cuts versus displacement}
\label{sec:results-wagemargin}

The incidence families reallocate displacement across workers; the wage-margin family instead moves the same shock to the wage margin. Every employee keeps their job, and earnings are removed through author-imposed occupation-level cuts proportional to C-AIOE or PSS, calibrated so that the aggregate employee-earnings loss equals the realised gross earnings loss of the paired central displacement draw---\genGrossCutSharePct~per cent of the baseline wage bill at seed 0 (recorded target share \genGrossCutShareRaw), larger than the 7 per cent head-count rate because group job-loss rates correlate with group pay; the equality is asserted in code. The PSS rows are a PSS-ranked wage-cut stress test, not the \citet{hosseinilichtinger2026barriers} labour-supply mechanism. Three contrasts stand out. First, under the simulated wage schedules the fiscal cost rises from \pounds \genCentralCostBn~billion to \pounds \genWageMarginCentralCostBn~billion (\pounds \genWageMarginPssCostBn~billion under the PSS gradient). Wage cuts remove earnings taxed at marginal income tax and National Insurance rates, whereas displacement removes whole earnings, including the part covered by allowances and thresholds, so it forgoes less tax per pound of lost earnings; that difference exceeds the extra means-tested benefit spending that displacement triggers. This comparison is conditional on these calibrations and does not establish a general lower bound.

Second, the poverty effect falls in the simulated wage-cut cases: the $\genCentralPovBhcSignedPp$-point BHC poverty change becomes $\genWageMarginCentralPovBhcSignedPp$ points under the C-AIOE gradient and $\genWageMarginPssPovBhcSignedPp$ under PSS. Spreading the calibrated loss across employees pushes fewer households across the frozen absolute line than concentrating losses on displaced workers; this does not establish a bound for other wage schedules.

Third, the inequality effect is smaller under both author-chosen wage gradients: the Gini changes by $\genWageMarginCentralGiniSignedPp$ and $\genWageMarginPssGiniSignedPp$ points, against $\genCentralGiniSignedPp$ points for displacement and $\genGridGiniMinSignedPp$ points for the capital-only grid cell. Percentage cuts scale with gradients concentrated in well-paid occupations and compress earnings from above, while the capital uplift widens the distribution. The comparison shows that poverty and inequality conclusions depend on both the adjustment margin and its imposed schedule; it does not establish a universal fiscal or distributional result.

\subsection{The age and gender dimensions}
\label{sec:results-agegender}

The incidence axis has a within-family age structure. Under exposure-proportional incidence, workers aged 16--24 account for \genAgeCentralYoungSharePct~per cent of the displaced in the central scenario (seed 0), because young people in the UK are concentrated in low-exposure service occupations; the largest displaced group is aged \genAgeCentralLargestBand, with \genAgeCentralLargestSharePct~per cent. The junior-concentrated family of Table~\ref{tab:incidence} reweights displacement probabilities towards junior workers while holding the aggregate displacement rate at 7 per cent. Because juniors are concentrated in occupation groups whose fixed quotas the tilt does not change, the shift is partial: the 16--24 share rises from \genAgeCentralYoungSharePct~to \genAgeYouthYoungSharePct~per cent and the 55--64 share moves from \genAgeCentralOlderSharePct~to \genAgeYouthOlderSharePct~per cent (seed 0), while the largest displaced group remains those aged \genAgeYouthLargestBand~(\genAgeYouthLargestSharePct~per cent). Over the 50 assignment draws the cost is \pounds \genIncJuniorCostMeanBn~$\pm$~\genIncJuniorCostSdBn~billion under the junior tilt against \pounds \genIncExposureCostMeanBn~$\pm$~\genIncExposureCostSdBn~billion under exposure-proportional incidence. The junior tilt is therefore an additional scenario assumption rather than a consequence of occupational exposure alone. % age shares: results/central.json, results/central_youth_tilted.json (seed 0)

If AI adoption in practice falls hardest on juniors through recruitment rather than dismissal---as the seniority-biased hiring evidence of \citet{hosseini2026} and the early-career employment declines in AI-exposed occupations documented by \citet{brynjolfsson2025} suggest---the fiscal consequences could differ materially from the broad-based scenarios above, because junior workers contribute little income tax and National Insurance. That channel, however, operates through hiring flows that stock-based simulation frameworks such as mine do not capture by construction.

Exposure also carries a gender gradient. Employed women have a mean C-AIOE of \genGenderFemaleExposure{} against \genGenderMaleExposure{} for employed men, reflecting women's concentration in administrative, professional and associate-professional work. In the central scenario this translates into a displacement rate of \genGenderFemaleRatePct~per cent for employed women against \genGenderMaleRatePct~per cent for men (means over 20 draws): women hold \genGenderFemaleEmpSharePct~per cent of employment and account for \genGenderFemaleDispSharePct~per cent of the displaced. In the seed-0 draw, household income, counted for every person, falls by \genGenderFemaleIncomeAbsPct~per cent for women and by \genGenderMaleIncomeAbsPct~per cent for men. % results/appendix/gender_incidence.json
A natural reading is that income pooling within couples mutes the individual asymmetry at the household level, though I have not decomposed the result and offer this as a hypothesis rather than a finding. The individual-level asymmetry itself echoes the international evidence that female employment is substantially more exposed to generative AI than male employment \citep{ilo2025}.

\subsection{Benefit caseloads}
\label{sec:results-caseloads}

The fiscal aggregates above can also be read on the delivery side, as caseloads. In the central scenario (seed 0) the shock draws \genCaseloadNewUcBenunitsK,000 benefit units into Universal Credit while \genCaseloadExitUcBenunitsK,000 exit, a net caseload increase of \genCaseloadNetNewUcBenunitsK,000 benefit units, and annual UC spending rises by \pounds \genCaseloadUcSpendChangeBn~billion from a baseline of \pounds \genCaseloadUcBaselineBn~billion, of which \pounds \genCaseloadHousingBn~billion is the housing element within paid awards. The high scenario adds \genCaseloadHighNetK,000 net new benefit units and \pounds \genCaseloadHighSpendBn~billion, and the low scenario \genCaseloadLowNetK,000 benefit units and \pounds \genCaseloadLowSpendBn~billion. Two accounting notes apply: only the benefit-unit figures are net of exits---the corresponding household and person counts (\genCaseloadNewUcHouseholdsK,000 and \genCaseloadNewUcPersonsK,000 in the central scenario) are gross new entrants---and the housing element is measured within paid awards, slightly overstating housing-specific cash. % results/caseloads/summary.csv
