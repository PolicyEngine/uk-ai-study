\section{Results}
\label{sec:results}

I first report the central scenario, how the tax-benefit system absorbs it and where in the income distribution the loss falls, then the two comparisons that organise the paper: who loses the job, and how the earnings loss arrives. The section closes with the assumptions that move the central cost and an account of where the displaced wage bill goes; Online Appendix~\ref{sec:results-obr} benchmarks the simulation against the OBR.

\subsection{The central scenario and its three channels}
\label{sec:results-channels}

Table~\ref{tab:presets} summarises the central scenario and the two presets. Over 50 assignment draws, the central shock displaces \genCentralMcDisplacedMeanM~million employees, costs the Exchequer \pounds\genCentralMcCostMeanBn~billion a year (draw standard deviation \pounds\genCentralMcCostSdBn~billion; standard error of the mean \pounds\genCentralMcCostSeBn~billion), and raises the Gini coefficient by \genCentralMcGiniMeanPp~points from a baseline of \genBaselineGini. The exposure-proportional rule concentrates job loss in higher-income households: \genTransDOnePct~per cent of people in the bottom decile move from employment to unemployment, against \genTransDTenPct~per cent in the top (Figure~\ref{fig:transition}). That gradient restates the allocation assumption.

The central cost nets three flows. Running each channel alone on 20 paired draws, displacement costs \pounds\genChannelMcDispCostAbsBn~billion, while the survivor-wage and capital channels each raise net receipts by \pounds\genChannelMcWageCostAbsBn~billion; the channels sum to within \pounds\genChannelMcInteractionCostAbsBn~billion of the full shock. Per displaced worker, the displacement channel costs \pounds\genCostPerDisplacedGbp{} (mean of the per-draw ratio over \genCostPerDisplacedDraws~draws, standard deviation \pounds\genCostPerDisplacedSdGbp). By tax and benefit, the 50-draw cost combines lost income tax on earnings, net of the tax on survivors' wage gains (\pounds\genCostOtherIncomeTaxAbsBn~billion), employer NI (\pounds\genCostEmployerNiAbsBn~billion), employee NI (\pounds\genCostEmployeeNiAbsBn~billion) and higher UC spending (\pounds\genCostUcAbsBn~billion), against higher dividend tax (\pounds\genCostDividendTaxAbsBn~billion), higher savings tax (\pounds\genCostSavingsTaxAbsBn~billion) and lower childcare support (\pounds\genCostChildcareAbsBn~billion). The Exchequer loses the employer contributions on displaced workers' earnings whatever their incidence; whether the saved labour cost returns as taxable profit is the closure question of Section~\ref{sec:results-closure}. For displaced public-sector employees the employer NI is a flow within government, which the cost nonetheless counts as lost. The government balance also omits the public sector's side of displacement: displaced public-sector employees cost \pounds\genCentralPaybillMeanBn~billion a year in pay, employer NI and employer pension contributions (mean over \genCentralPaybillDraws~draws, standard deviation \pounds\genCentralPaybillSdBn~billion; Table~\ref{tab:presets}). Whether that sum is a saving depends on whether the services they provided continue, which the simulation does not model.

The Gini rises because displaced workers lose all earnings, survivors gain, and the capital uplift accrues mainly in the top decile, which holds \genCapTopSharePct~per cent of baseline capital income. The rise does not depend on the capital channel: on its own the displacement channel raises the Gini by \genChannelMcDispGiniAbsPp~points (20 draws), and with the capital shock switched off the Gini rises in all \genAppGridNoCapGiniPositive{} shocked cells of the grid (Online Appendix~\ref{app:grid}).

\paragraph{Poverty.} Absolute BHC poverty rises by \genCentralMcPovBhcMeanPp~points (standard error \genCentralMcPovBhcSePp). Because every displaced worker loses a full year of earnings, I read this as an upper bound for exposure-proportional incidence under these survivor-wage and capital assumptions and the model's benefit rules; other displacement rules at the same rate give larger rises (Table~\ref{tab:incidence}). The absolute line is frozen at its baseline value; on the relative line, which moves with the shocked median, the central rise is \genCentralMcRelPovBhcMeanPp~points. At seed~0, \genPovInDisplacedHhPct~per cent of people entering absolute poverty live with a displaced worker, and \genPovSoleEarnerPct~per cent of those households had no other earner.

\paragraph{A historical benchmark.} The framework over-predicts the poverty response to a past downturn. A backcast displaces 2.6 per cent of employees uniformly, matching the rise in the Labour Force Survey unemployment rate over 2008--2010, with no wage or capital channel (means over five draws). It raises simulated absolute BHC poverty by $\genBackcastPovBhcPp$ points, absolute AHC poverty by $\genBackcastPovAhcPp$ points and the Gini by $\genBackcastGiniPp$ points, and changes relative BHC poverty by $\genBackcastRelPovPp$ points. Recorded HBAI outturns over 2007/08--2010/11 show absolute BHC poverty and the Gini roughly flat to slightly falling and relative poverty falling: benefits were uprated with high inflation while median incomes fell, and unemployment spells were of mixed length. The gap combines the channels outside the simulation (uprating, duration, behavioural and macro-policy responses) with model and data error and the composition of the 2008--10 job losers. Per point of uniform displacement the backcast gives $\genBackcastPerPointPp$ points of BHC poverty, against $\genUniformPerPointPp$ for the uniform 7 per cent rule, which also carries the wage and capital channels.
% results/robustness/recession_backcast.json

\paragraph{An OBR benchmark.} Sized to the OBR's March 2026 technological-displacement scenario, a \genObrUnempRisePp-point rise in unemployment with no survivor-wage or capital change, the simulation costs \pounds\genObrCostMeanBn~billion a year (\genObrDraws~draws), against an average of \pounds\genObrBorrowingBn~billion of higher borrowing in the OBR scenario \citep[para.~6.18]{obr2026}. The totals agree because offsetting lines differ: the simulation loses more receipts (\pounds\genObrReceiptsBn~billion against the OBR's \pounds\genObrReceiptsLostBn~billion) and adds less welfare spending (\pounds\genObrWelfareBn~billion against \pounds\genObrWelfareAddedBn~billion). The two figures also differ in timing and coverage; Online Appendix~\ref{sec:results-obr} compares them line by line.

\paragraph{What a displaced worker can claim.} A displaced employee meets the UK system through two routes. New-style Jobseeker's Allowance, a contributory benefit, pays \pounds\genJsaContribWeekGbp{} a week to a claimant aged 25 or over for up to 26 weeks; the model pays it only where the survey reports an award, so under the central assumption newly displaced workers do not receive it. UC is means-tested on the benefit unit. In \genSimTaxYear{} its standard allowance is \pounds\genUcSingleOldMonthGbp{} a month for a single claimant aged 25 or over and \pounds\genUcCoupleOldMonthGbp{} for a couple with one partner aged 25 or over, plus elements for children, housing, disability and childcare. A partner's earnings above the work allowance (\pounds\genUcWorkAllowanceHousingGbp{} a month with housing support and \pounds\genUcWorkAllowanceNoHousingGbp{} without, for claimants with children or limited capability for work) reduce the award by \genUcTaperPct~pence in the pound, and a benefit unit with capital above \pounds\genUcCapitalLimitGbp{} has no entitlement. Each benefit unit keeps the UC take-up decision drawn for it in the baseline data. With displacement sized as in the OBR benchmark (Online Appendix~\ref{sec:results-obr}), that decision is to claim for \genTkFlagTruePct~per cent of the displaced workers' benefit units and for \genTkFlagTrueEntitledPct~per cent of those entitled to UC once displaced. Two variants, reported beside the central results, set take-up to one for every benefit unit with a displaced worker and add new-style JSA. A displaced sole earner with no other income and capital below the limit is entitled to the full standard allowance and, if renting, the housing element; a partner's earnings and the household's capital enter the same test.

\paragraph{Caseloads.} In the seed-0 central draw the shock draws \genCaseloadNewUcBenunitsK,000 benefit units into UC while \genCaseloadExitUcBenunitsK,000 exit, a net increase of \genCaseloadNetNewUcBenunitsK,000, against \genCentralDisplacedTwoM~million displaced workers. Annual UC spending rises by \pounds\genCaseloadUcSpendChangeBn~billion from a baseline of \pounds\genCaseloadUcBaselineBn~billion, of which \pounds\genCaseloadHousingBn~billion is the housing element within paid awards. The high preset adds \genCaseloadHighNetK,000 net benefit units and \pounds\genCaseloadHighSpendBn~billion, the low preset \genCaseloadLowNetK,000 and \pounds\genCaseloadLowSpendBn~billion, and the junior-concentrated rule \genCaseloadJuniorNetK,000 and \pounds\genCaseloadJuniorSpendBn~billion. Only the benefit-unit figures are net of exits; the corresponding counts of households (\genCaseloadNewUcHouseholdsK,000) and persons (\genCaseloadNewUcPersonsK,000) are gross new entrants. % results/caseloads/summary.csv

\subsection{Where the income loss falls}
\label{sec:results-deciles}

Averaged over 20 displacement draws, with each household weighted by its number of members, the central-scenario change in disposable income by baseline decile is hump-shaped (Online Appendix Figure~\ref{fig:decomp-ci}): the largest mean loss, \genDecompCiMaxLossPct~per cent, falls in decile~\genDecompCiMaxLossDecile, because displacement losses concentrate in the upper-middle deciles while capital-income gains offset them at the top, where the change is $\genDecompCiDTenSignedPct$ per cent (95 per cent interval for the mean $[\genDecompCiDTenLoPct, \genDecompCiDTenHiPct]$). The bottom-decile result depends on how households are ranked. Under the HBAI ranking decile~1 changes by $\genDecileRankHbaiDOneSignedPct$ per cent; ranked on income before pension contributions it changes by $\genDecileRankPrePensionDOneSignedPct$ per cent, and excluding the \genDecileRankExclHouseholds~households whose personal pension contributions exceed both \pounds\genDecileRankLargePpcFloorGbp{} and their earnings it changes by $\genDecileRankExclDOneSignedPct$ per cent. Those households carry high weights, sit in decile~1 under HBAI and receive capital income, so the bottom-decile change \genDecileOneRobustness, and I do not report it as a finding.

The household-basis share is not comparable with the unemployment-shock coefficients of \citet{dolls2012}, whose working paper reports \genDollsUkTotalPct~per cent for the UK, \genDollsUkBenefitsPct~points of it from benefits \citep[Table~3]{dolls2010wp}. Four differences separate the designs. First, take-up: they assume full take-up, whereas here the displaced benefit units entitled to UC claim it at the rate reported in Section~\ref{sec:results-channels}, against \genTkUnempFlagEntitledPct~per cent of entitled units with an unemployed member in the baseline. Second, who becomes unemployed: they reweight the sample towards people who are already unemployed, whereas the displaced here lose \pounds\genObrMeanLostEarningsGbp{} of earnings on average, and of their benefit units \genTkOverCapitalPct~per cent hold capital above the UC limit, \genTkOtherEarnerPct~per cent have another earner and \genTkOwnerPct~per cent own their home. Third, contributory benefits: reweighting gives the added unemployed the unemployment insurance that the survey's unemployed report, whereas the simulation pays no new-style JSA to the newly displaced. Fourth, incidence: exposure-proportional displacement places most of the market-income loss in the upper half of the distribution (Table~\ref{tab:stabilisation}). The comparison therefore neither confirms nor contradicts their result.

Two variants remove the first and third differences for this shock (\genObrDraws~draws each, paired with the same displaced workers). Setting UC take-up to one for every benefit unit with a displaced worker, in a paired baseline and in the shock, raises the UC response to \pounds\genTkObrTakeupUcBn~billion. Adding 26 weeks of new-style JSA for displaced workers aged 18--65 who meet the contribution condition, \genTkObrJsaEligiblePct~per cent of them, pays \pounds\genTkObrJsaJsaBn~billion of JSA; UC counts JSA as unearned income, so the UC response is \pounds\genTkObrJsaUcBn~billion, below the take-up-only figure, and the model \genTkJsaTaxWords. Cash benefits then rise by \genTkObrTakeupCashPence~and \genTkObrJsaCashPence~pence per pound of earnings lost.

\subsection{Who loses the job}
\label{sec:results-incidence}

Table~\ref{tab:incidence} reallocates the same expected number of displaced workers across the five rules of Section~\ref{sec:incidence}; every non-displaced person's wage change and every person's capital-income change are identical across rules. Across the four stylised rules the Exchequer cost runs from \pounds\genIncUniformCostMeanBn~billion (uniform) to \pounds\genIncCompressionCostMeanBn~billion (expertise compression); the top-loaded stress test costs \pounds\genIncKleinCostMeanBn~billion. The cost tracks the earnings of those displaced. Adding the survivor-wage and capital channels back, the displacement channel costs about \pounds\genFamPerWorkerUniformGbp{} per displaced worker under uniform incidence, \pounds\genFamPerWorkerExposureGbp{} under exposure-proportional, \pounds\genFamPerWorkerCompressionGbp{} under expertise compression and \pounds\genFamPerWorkerKleinGbp{} under the stress test (means of per-draw ratios: 50-draw costs plus the 20-draw channel receipts, assuming the channels add up as they do in the central case).

Because the rules share assignment seeds, I compare them draw by draw; the rules select different workers within a seed, so the pairing removes little of the draw noise. Uniform incidence costs \pounds\genPairUniExpCostMeanAbs~billion less than exposure-proportional incidence (paired 95 per cent interval $[\genPairUniExpCostLo, \genPairUniExpCostHi]$) and expertise compression \pounds\genPairCompExpCostMeanAbs~billion more ($[\genPairCompExpCostLo, \genPairCompExpCostHi]$); the junior tilt lowers the cost by \pounds\genPairJunExpCostMeanAbs~billion, an interval that includes zero ($[\genPairJunExpCostLo, \genPairJunExpCostHi]$), because the tilt operates within fixed occupation quotas and young workers sit mostly in low-exposure groups. These intervals describe assignment noise across 50 draws and narrow as draws are added; they carry no information about survey sampling or about which rule is right.

Uniform incidence is the exception to the cost ordering. Relative to exposure-proportional incidence, it raises BHC poverty by a further $\genPairUniExpPovMean$ points ($[\genPairUniExpPovLo, \genPairUniExpPovHi]$) and the Gini by $\genPairUniExpGiniMean$ points while costing less; after housing costs the two rules' poverty rises (\genIncUniformPovAhcMeanPp~and \genIncExposurePovAhcMeanPp~points, Table~\ref{tab:incidence}) differ by less than the draw noise. Expertise compression raises BHC poverty by $\genPairCompExpPovMean$ points more ($[\genPairCompExpPovLo, \genPairCompExpPovHi]$) while costing more. Two routes could make both the cheapest rule and the costlier high-earner tilt raise poverty by more than exposure-proportional incidence. Uniform incidence displaces more workers in lower-income households, which start closer to the line; the high-earner tilts displace more sole earners, whose households fall the full distance. The saved draws do not separate the two routes.

Figure~\ref{fig:incidence} shows the decile pattern behind these differences. Under every rule the share of each decile moving to unemployment rises with income, because employees concentrate in the higher deciles. Uniform incidence moves a larger share of the lower and middle deciles to unemployment than exposure-proportional incidence, and the top-loaded stress test a larger share of the top decile. The right panel places each rule's cost against its poverty change on the same single draw.

\paragraph{Age and gender.} Young workers are concentrated in low-exposure occupations: \genAgeYoungLowTertilePct~per cent of employees aged 16--24 work in the lowest exposure tertile. \subsection{How the earnings loss arrives}
\label{sec:results-wagemargin}

Table~\ref{tab:wagemargin} moves the same gross earnings loss from job loss to wage cuts over \genWcDraws~paired draws. Each wage-cut draw removes the earnings of the workers displaced in the central draw with the same seed, on average \genWcGrossCutSharePct~per cent of the baseline employee wage bill. On these seeds job loss costs \pounds\genWcJobCostMeanTwoDpBn~billion and raises absolute BHC poverty by \genWcJobPovBhcMeanPp~points. Delivered as occupation-level wage cuts, the same loss costs \pounds\genWcWageCostMeanTwoDpBn~billion, \pounds\genWcPairCostDiffTwoDpBn~billion more (standard error of the paired difference \pounds\genWcPairCostDiffSeBn~billion; at least \pounds\genWcPairCostDiffMinBn~billion more in every draw), and raises BHC poverty by \genWcWagePovBhcMeanPp~points (at least \genWcPairPovBhcDiffSmallestAbsPp~points less than job loss in every draw). The survivor-wage uplift and the capital shock are on in both rows.

The margin changes what each lost pound costs the Exchequer. Employers pay NI on top of earnings, so the cost per pound has two parts: the household basis of Section~\ref{sec:microsim}, which is the part of the lost pound that income tax, employee NI and benefits absorb, and employer NI. Displacement alone, with no wage or capital channel, costs the government balance \genAbsJobGovDrawsPct~pence per pound of earnings removed (\genWcDraws~draws, range \genAbsJobGovDrawsMinPct--\genAbsJobGovDrawsMaxPct~pence). At central scale the household-basis split of displacement alone is available for seed~0 only, where job loss costs \genAbsJobGovPct~pence: \genAbsJobHhPct~pence on the household basis, in line with Table~\ref{tab:stabilisation}, and \genAbsJobRestPct~pence of employer NI. The same gross loss delivered as wage cuts alone costs \genAbsCutGovPct~pence: \genAbsCutHhPct~pence on the household basis and \genAbsCutErNiPct~pence of employer NI (\genWcDraws~draws). Employer NI is of similar size on the two margins, so the gap between them arises in income tax, employee NI and benefits. Measured on HBAI disposable income, households bear \genObrHhBearPct~pence of each pound lost through job loss in the OBR-sized run (Table~\ref{tab:stabilisation}, where lost pension contributions also count) and \genWcCutOnlyHhBearPct~pence of each pound lost through the central-sized wage cuts (\genWcDraws~draws). With every displaced benefit unit claiming UC, job loss costs \genAbsJobGovTakeupPct~pence on the government balance and \genAbsJobHhTakeupPct~pence on the household basis, and with new-style JSA as well \genAbsJobGovJsaPct~and \genAbsJobHhJsaPct~pence; both remain below the wage-cut figures. I do not apply full take-up to the wage-cut runs, where it could only add UC spending. The ordering of the two margins is a result for this shock rather than a property of the UK system: its job losses fall on workers with above-average earnings, and many of them have capital, working partners or owned homes, and its wage cuts are graded to exposure, so they fall on earnings taxed at higher marginal rates. A wage cut removes pounds taxed at the worker's marginal rates of income tax and NI, and for UC claimants adds UC at the taper rate; job loss removes whole earnings, including the slice covered by the personal allowance and NI thresholds, and the UC it adds is less than the tax it forgoes. The gap therefore narrows with shorter spells, because job loss costs an average rate on full-year earnings while wage cuts fall at the margin: the run in which displaced workers keep half their annual earnings (Table~\ref{tab:breakeven}) implies a cost per pound most of the way from the job-loss to the wage-cut figure. Poverty moves the other way: concentrated losses push displaced households across the line, while the same loss spread across employees outside the least-exposed group moves few households below it. Without the survivor-wage uplift and the capital shock, the wage cuts raise BHC poverty by \genWcCutOnlyPovBhcMeanPp~points and change the Gini by $\genWcCutOnlyGiniMeanSignedPp$ points (\genWcDraws~draws), because the cuts are graded to exposure and exposed occupations pay more; the Gini rise in the wage-cut rows of Table~\ref{tab:wagemargin} comes from the other two channels. Displacement alone raises BHC poverty by \genChannelMcDispPovBhcAbsPp~points on the same seeds.

Table~\ref{tab:mixedwage} holds the gross loss fixed and moves it between margins on the seed-0 draw, whose BHC poverty rise and Gini rise (\genSeedZeroExposureGiniPp~points against a 50-draw mean of \genCentralMcGiniMeanPp) are each the second lowest of the 50 exposure-proportional draws and whose cost sits close to the 50-draw mean. As the displaced share $\lambda$ rises from 0 to 1, the cost falls from \pounds\genMixedZeroCostBn~billion to \pounds\genMixedOneCostBn~billion and BHC poverty rises from $\genMixedZeroPovBhcPp$ to $\genMixedOnePovBhcPp$ points. The second panel shows the survivor-wage assumption: with no pass-through to survivors the central cost is \pounds\genSurvivorZeroCostBn~billion, and a 2.6 per cent survivor-wage decline gives \pounds\genSurvivorMinusTwoCostBn~billion.

\subsection{Where the displaced wage bill goes}
\label{sec:results-closure}

The central scenario imposes the capital uplift independently of the labour shock, and its government balance counts no corporation tax. Table~\ref{tab:closure} replaces the uplift with an explicit account of where the displaced wage bill goes, on the seed-0 draw where every flow is available. Displaced workers lose \pounds\genFaWageBillBn~billion of earnings and survivors gain \pounds\genFaSurvivorGainBn~billion, so labour income falls by \pounds\genFaNetLabourBn~billion. The central scenario instead adds \pounds\genFaCapUpliftBn~billion of household capital income, \pounds\genFaDivUpliftBn~billion of it dividends and the rest interest.

In the closed account a share $\phi$ of the net labour-income fall reappears as company profit, which pays corporation tax at 25 per cent; companies pay out \genFaPayoutPct~per cent of post-tax profit, and a share $s$ of that reaches UK households, the remainder going to overseas owners and pension funds, whose returns the household simulation does not tax. Household dividends pay tax at the \genFaDivTaxRatePct~per cent average rate the simulated capital channel implies. The interest uplift has no counterpart in this account. The closed balance keeps the displacement and survivor-wage channels and their interaction and replaces the capital channel with corporation tax and dividend tax on the implied payout.
