\section{Conclusions}
\label{sec:discussion}

The results are scenario analysis. I do not forecast the pace or extent of AI adoption; I trace what the UK tax-benefit system would do if labour-market shocks of a given size, incidence and margin occurred under current rules. The observational record does not yet discriminate between the incidence views: the earliest evidence ranges from minimal effects on earnings and hours \citep{humlum2025} to relative employment declines for young workers in exposed occupations \citep{brynjolfsson2025}, none at the scale of the central shock.

Three findings hold across the central parameters examined. First, who loses the job moves the cost of a fixed head-count shock by \pounds\genIncStylisedCostSpanBn~billion across the stylised rules, a difference that does not depend on the survivor-wage and capital receipts the rules share, because the Exchequer loses each displaced worker's income tax and NI and the UK tax base leans on the earnings of the occupations most exposed. Second, the margin changes how the shock is shared: for this shock the Exchequer absorbs more of each pound lost through wage cuts (\genAbsWageGovPct~pence) than through job loss (\genAbsJobGovPct~pence, or \genAbsJobGovJsaPct~pence if every displaced worker claims UC and new-style JSA), while job loss concentrates the household loss and raises absolute poverty by \genCentralPovBhcPp~points against \genWageMarginCentralPovBhcAbsPp~under wage cuts. Third, measured inequality rises under every displacement rule and grid cell examined, with or without the capital channel. The level and sign of the central cost, by contrast, turn on three assumptions with break-even values close to their central settings (Table~\ref{tab:breakeven}) and on how much of the displaced wage bill returns to UK households as taxable profit (Table~\ref{tab:closure}).

The comparison with the Irish simulation of \citet{doorley2026}, which uses the same central conventions on SILC data and the SWITCH model, shows the same qualitative pattern (Table~\ref{tab:ireland}). The fiscal magnitudes are not comparable, because \citet{doorley2026} report Exchequer effects as shares of a net revenue base that includes welfare spending.

\subsection{Limitations and further work}
\label{sec:limitations}

\paragraph{Exposure.} Exposure varies across nine SOC2020 major groups through a chained crosswalk, a reconstructed complementarity index and ASHE weights covering 340 of 412 unit groups; the occupation of SPI-drawn top-income records is imputed. Quota rates run from zero (elementary occupations) to about 10 per cent (Table~\ref{tab:jobloss}), so incidence is a between-group gradient. A UK-native index \citep{henseke2025} with detailed occupations imputed from the Labour Force Survey would remove both constraints.

\paragraph{Dynamics and behaviour.} Displacement is a one-year stock with full-year zero earnings, no re-employment, no change in partners' hours, no redundancy pay and no running down of savings. UK evidence on earnings losses after displacement \citep{upward2019} would calibrate spell length and re-employment wages, and US evidence shows workers from AI-exposed occupations gaining from publicly funded retraining \citep{hyman2025}, a margin the framework does not model. Under the central assumption newly displaced workers receive no new-style JSA, because the model carries only reported awards, and keep their benefit units' UC take-up flags, which are random draws rather than modelled claiming decisions; Section~\ref{sec:results-deciles} reports runs that relax both. Council tax reduction does not respond to the loss of earnings in the model, and the benefit cap applies from the start of the claim, without the nine-month grace period that follows a year of steady work. Reduced hiring of young workers, the margin on which the strongest early evidence lies \citep{brynjolfsson2025, kleinteeselink2025, hosseini2026}, is outside a stock-based framework.

\paragraph{Capital and the self-employed.} The capital channel covers interest and dividends only, and its size depends on the dividend base (Section~\ref{sec:results-breakeven}). The self-employed are outside the labour-market shocks.
