\section{Conclusions}
\label{sec:discussion}

This paper asks how much of an AI-driven labour-market shock the UK tax-benefit system absorbs and who bears the rest. The results are scenario analysis, not a forecast: they trace what the UK rules legislated or announced for \genSimTaxYear{} would do if shocks of a given size, incidence and margin occurred. The earliest evidence ranges from minimal effects on earnings and hours \citep{humlum2025} to relative employment declines for young workers in exposed occupations \citep{brynjolfsson2025}, and cannot yet tell the incidence views apart.

Holding the size of the shock fixed, three results hold across the central parameters. First, who loses the job matters for the public finances. Moving the same expected number of displaced workers across the four stylised rules changes the annual Exchequer cost by \pounds\genIncStylisedCostSpanBn~billion: each displaced worker takes their income tax and NI with them, and the UK tax base leans on the earnings of the occupations most exposed to AI.

Second, for this shock the margin of adjustment changes how the loss is shared. Per pound of earnings lost, the Exchequer bears \genAbsCutGovPct~pence under wage cuts and \genAbsJobGovDrawsPct~pence under job loss, employer NI included (both over \genWcDraws~draws). If every displaced worker's benefit unit claims UC and eligible displaced workers receive new-style JSA, the seed-0 job-loss figure rises from \genAbsJobGovPct~to \genAbsJobGovJsaPct~pence, still below the wage-cut figure. Job loss, however, concentrates the loss on fewer households: with the survivor-wage and capital channels on in both, it raises absolute poverty by \genWcJobPovBhcMeanPp~points, against \genWcWagePovBhcMeanPp~under wage cuts of the same gross size. Third, with job loss measured inequality rises under every displacement rule and in every shocked grid cell, including the exposure-proportional grid without the capital channel, while wage cuts on their own lower it.

The level and sign of the central cost turn on three assumptions, each with a break-even value (Table~\ref{tab:breakeven}), and on how much of the displaced wage bill returns to UK households as taxable profit (Table~\ref{tab:closure}).

The framework has limits that point to further work. Exposure varies only across the nine SOC2020 major groups, so incidence is a between-group gradient; a UK-native index \citep{henseke2025} with detailed occupations imputed from the Labour Force Survey would remove that constraint. Displacement is a one-year stock: displaced workers earn nothing for the full year, with no re-employment, change in partners' hours, redundancy pay or running down of savings. UK evidence on earnings losses after displacement \citep{upward2019} could calibrate spell length and re-employment wages. Under the central assumption newly displaced workers receive no new-style JSA and keep their benefit units' randomly drawn UC take-up flags; Section~\ref{sec:results-deciles} reports runs that relax both. Council tax reduction does not respond to the loss of earnings, and the benefit cap applies from the start of the claim, with no nine-month grace period. Reduced hiring of young workers, the margin the early evidence emphasises \citep{brynjolfsson2025, kleinteeselink2025, hosseini2026}, and retraining \citep{hyman2025} lie outside the framework. The capital channel covers interest and dividends only, and the self-employed are outside the labour-market shocks.
