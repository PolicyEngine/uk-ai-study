\section{Conclusions}
\label{sec:discussion}

This paper asks how much of an AI-driven labour-market shock the UK tax-benefit system absorbs and who bears the rest. The results are scenario analysis, not a forecast of the pace or extent of AI adoption: they trace what current rules would do if shocks of a given size, incidence and margin occurred. The observational record cannot yet tell the incidence views apart. The earliest evidence ranges from minimal effects on earnings and hours \citep{humlum2025} to relative employment declines for young workers in exposed occupations \citep{brynjolfsson2025}, and none of it approaches the scale of the central shock.

Holding the size of the shock fixed, three results hold across the central parameters examined. First, who loses the job matters for the public finances. Moving the same expected number of displaced workers across the four stylised rules changes the annual Exchequer cost by \pounds\genIncStylisedCostSpanBn~billion, a difference that arises from who is displaced, since the rules share every survivor's wage change and everyone's capital-income change. Each displaced worker takes their income tax and NI with them, and the UK tax base leans on the earnings of the occupations most exposed to AI.

Second, for this shock the margin of adjustment changes how the loss is shared. Per pound of earnings lost, the Exchequer bears \genAbsCutGovPct~pence when the loss comes through wage cuts and \genAbsJobGovPct~pence when it comes through job loss, employer NI included (\genAbsCutHhPct~and \genAbsJobHhPct~pence on the household basis). If every displaced worker's benefit unit claims UC and eligible displaced workers receive new-style JSA, the job-loss figures rise to \genAbsJobGovJsaPct~and \genAbsJobHhJsaPct~pence, still below the wage-cut figures. Job loss, however, concentrates the loss on fewer households: with the survivor-wage and capital channels on in both, it raises absolute poverty by \genWcJobPovBhcMeanPp~points, against \genWcWagePovBhcMeanPp~under wage cuts of the same gross size. Third, with job loss measured inequality rises under every displacement rule and in every shocked grid cell, and still does so in the exposure-proportional grid without the capital channel, while wage cuts on their own lower it.

The level and sign of the central cost, by contrast, turn on three assumptions whose break-even values lie close to their central settings (Table~\ref{tab:breakeven}) and on how much of the displaced wage bill returns to UK households as taxable profit (Table~\ref{tab:closure}). The Irish simulation of \citet{doorley2026}, which applies the same central conventions to SILC data with the SWITCH model, shows the same qualitative pattern of displacement incidence and Gini response; its fiscal magnitudes are not comparable (Online Appendix~\ref{app:ireland}).

The framework has limits that point to further work. Exposure varies only across the nine SOC2020 major groups, and the measure rests on a chained crosswalk, a reconstructed complementarity index and ASHE weights covering 340 of 412 unit groups; the occupation of SPI-drawn top-income records is imputed. Quota rates run from zero for elementary occupations to about 10 per cent (Table~\ref{tab:jobloss}), so incidence is a between-group gradient. A UK-native index \citep{henseke2025}, combined with detailed occupations imputed from the Labour Force Survey, would remove both constraints.

Displacement is a one-year stock: displaced workers earn nothing for the full year, and there is no re-employment, no change in partners' hours, no redundancy pay and no running down of savings. UK evidence on earnings losses after displacement \citep{upward2019} could calibrate spell length and re-employment wages, and US evidence that workers from AI-exposed occupations gain from publicly funded retraining \citep{hyman2025} points to a margin the framework does not model. Under the central assumption newly displaced workers receive no new-style JSA, because the model carries only reported awards, and keep their benefit units' UC take-up flags, which are random draws rather than modelled claiming decisions; Section~\ref{sec:results-deciles} reports runs that relax both. In the model, council tax reduction does not respond to the loss of earnings, and the benefit cap applies from the start of the claim, without the nine-month grace period that follows a year of steady work. Reduced hiring of young workers, the margin the early evidence emphasises \citep{brynjolfsson2025, kleinteeselink2025, hosseini2026}, lies outside a stock-based framework. Finally, the capital channel covers interest and dividends only, and its size depends on the dividend base (Section~\ref{sec:results-breakeven}); the self-employed are outside the labour-market shocks.
