\section{Conclusions and policy implications}
\label{sec:discussion}

The results should be read as scenario analysis, not prediction. I do not forecast the pace or extent of AI adoption in the United Kingdom; I trace the distributional and fiscal consequences that would follow \emph{if} labour-market shocks of a given size and occupational incidence were to materialise, under tax-benefit rules as legislated. The observational record remains too thin to adjudicate between the incidence families: the earliest evidence ranges from minimal measured earnings and hours effects to date at one extreme to a modest relative contraction in exposed occupations at the other, none yet at the scale of my central shock. That unsettled state is the case for spanning the disagreement rather than resolving it by assumption, and what the paper contributes is not a claim about which world will arrive but a quantification of what turns on the answer.

The results can be benchmarked against the one directly comparable exercise, the Irish tax-benefit simulation of \citet{doorley2026}, which uses the same central conventions (7 per cent displacement, 2.6 per cent survivor-wage growth, +0.4 points on the return to capital) on Irish SILC data. Table~\ref{tab:ireland} compares the two. In both countries displacement concentrates in upper-income households, the tax-benefit system absorbs part of the loss, and measured inequality rises in every scenario. The decile profiles differ: in the UK simulation the capital uplift offsets the top decile's earnings loss on average, so the comparison of absorption uses the hardest-hit decile. The fiscal magnitudes are not directly comparable because \citet{doorley2026} report Exchequer effects only relative to a net-of-welfare revenue base.

Two structural features give the simulated displacement results their shape. Means-tested support and lower tax and National Insurance liabilities absorb part of the displaced workers' losses, and that cushioning appears in the public finances (Section~\ref{sec:results-channels} gives the cost by component). Inequality rises because zero earnings for selected workers, partial tax-benefit replacement, gains for survivors and the capital uplift jointly widen post-shock income gaps. This explanation is conditional on the simulated channels; it is not caused mechanically by the Gini ``re-ranking'' households.

\subsection{Policy implications: what depends on who bears the shock}

The central policy implication is conditional on incidence. Measured inequality rises in each simulated displacement family, while the balance between fiscal and poverty effects varies across the specified allocations.

If the shock is top-loaded, as in the exposure-proportional family, displaced workers are drawn from occupations that pay well and are taxed heavily, so each displacement removes income tax and National Insurance while adding comparatively little to means-tested expenditure. The Exchequer cost of the central shock is \pounds \genIncExposureCostMeanBn~billion under exposure-proportional incidence against \pounds \genIncUniformCostMeanBn~billion under uniform incidence (50-draw means), a paired uniform-minus-exposure difference of \pounds$\genPairUniExpCostMean$~billion for the same aggregate shock. Because both figures net a large displacement cost against receipts from the survivor-wage and capital channels, I compare them as differences in pounds, not as ratios. Under top-loaded incidence revenue resilience becomes relevant: the UK tax base leans on the taxation of labour income, and the workers most exposed are those who contribute most to it. In the simulation the assumed capital-income uplift raises receipts, because interest and dividends are taxed at the household level; the erosion risk concerns income that moves to lightly taxed or untaxed forms, a possibility that has motivated recent work on the taxation of capital in an AI-intensive economy \citep{bastani2024}. The tax-composition exercise of Section~\ref{sec:policy} quantifies one version of that possibility: whether base erosion is a first-order fiscal problem depends on how much of the displaced wage bill reappears as taxable corporate profit.

Under flatter or junior-concentrated incidence the balance between fiscal and poverty effects shifts. In the paired draw-level comparisons of Section~\ref{sec:results-incidence}, the junior-minus-exposure cost difference is \pounds$\genPairJunExpCostMean$~billion (\genPairJunExpCostSig), and the uniform-minus-exposure Gini and poverty differences are $\genPairUniExpGiniMean$ points (\genPairUniExpGiniSig) and $\genPairUniExpPovMean$ points (\genPairUniExpPovSig); these are scenario comparisons across shared assignment draws, not population-inference tests. % paired comparisons recomputed from results/robustness/incidence_draws_five.csv
Out-of-work support and labour-market entry are relevant to these scenarios. One limitation deserves emphasis. The strongest early empirical evidence locates AI's employment effects among junior workers \emph{within} exposed occupations and firms---relative employment declines of about 13 per cent for the youngest workers in the most exposed US occupations \citep{brynjolfsson2025} and UK job cuts concentrated almost entirely in junior roles \citep{kleinteeselink2025, hosseini2026}---operating chiefly through reduced hiring. A stock-based, occupation-proportional simulation cannot capture that margin by construction: allocating displacement across occupations makes 16--24-year-olds about \genAgeCentralYoungSharePct~per cent of the displaced, because they are under-represented in high-exposure professional occupations. The framework therefore likely understates youth incidence. The junior-concentrated family tilts displacement towards young workers within fixed occupation quotas, but it does not model reduced hiring. My one-year reweighting exercise also does not quantify dynamic scarring or persistent earnings effects of delayed entry.

For the simulated designs, Table~\ref{tab:policy} reports the cost per poverty point averted for the UC-side reforms and the wage-insurance specification. This ratio measures one objective, poverty reduction, and does not establish an overall instrument ranking: wage insurance serves different insurance and consumption-smoothing aims, and administrative, behavioural and financing effects are outside the comparison.

The expertise-compression and Klein-anchored families cost \pounds \genIncCompressionCostMeanBn~billion and \pounds \genIncKleinCostMeanBn~billion within the author-chosen calibrations. This is compatible with \citet{hosseinilichtinger2026barriers}, whose labour-supply channel concerns between-occupation wages rather than the household displacement exercise here. In the separate wage-margin sensitivity, my author-imposed PSS-proportional schedule raises the fiscal cost to \pounds \genWageMarginPssCostBn~billion, reduces the BHC poverty change to $\genWageMarginPssPovBhcSignedPp$ points and leaves a Gini change of $\genWageMarginPssGiniSignedPp$ points. These results show how outcomes vary across the schedules simulated here; they do not establish policy dominance or general upper and lower bounds.

Two considerations recur across the simulated displacement families. First, targeted retraining is relevant to the displaced: \citet{hyman2025} report rising returns to publicly funded retraining for workers from highly AI-exposed occupations in the United States, largely through moves into less-exposed work. Whether this finding transports to the UK is not tested here. Second, measured inequality rises in every displacement family examined; this is a conditional model result rather than a forecast.

Across the simulated displacement scenarios, the tax-benefit system partly cushions market-income losses at an incidence-dependent fiscal cost. The results point to three areas for further analysis rather than a settled policy prescription: occupational reallocation, entry prospects for younger workers, and the resilience of a labour-income-heavy tax base.

\subsection{Limitations and further work}
\label{sec:limitations}

Several limitations qualify the results; I state them by pipeline stage, with the measurement conventions needed to compare my figures with official statistics.

The first stage is the data and its weighting. All results use the HBAI equivalised household disposable income concept, with the Gini computed after bottom-coding negative incomes at zero. My baseline Gini of \genBaselineGini{} is below the official HBAI figure of about 0.34; the two differ in microdata, weighting and year, so levels are not comparable even though the changes I report are computed on a consistent basis. Microcosm's calibration brings employment, earnings, benefit caseloads and SPI income totals close to administrative figures, but it does so through uneven weights, and the occupation of the SPI-drawn top-income records is imputed rather than observed. Single-draw figures carry displacement-draw noise that is wider on these weights than on survey grossing weights (Section~\ref{sec:microsim}); the Monte Carlo means are the more reliable summaries (seed conventions in Appendix~\ref{app:seeds}).

Occupational exposure is assigned at the 1-digit SOC level, because the dataset's observed occupation field is at the major-group level, so within-group heterogeneity in exposure \citep{felten2021, webb2019, eloundou2023} is averaged away. The scores also reach the microdata through a reconstructed pipeline---a rebuilt complementarity adjustment and a chained SOC2018--SOC2010--ISCO--SOC2020 crosswalk with ASHE weights for 340 of 412 unit groups. Adopting the UK-native GAISI index \citep{henseke2025} together with detailed-occupation imputation from the Labour Force Survey would remove both the crosswalk and the resolution constraint, and is my first priority; for the expertise-compression family specifically, the public occupation-level expertise measures of \citet{hosseinilichtinger2026barriers} are already used as the PSS gradient of the wage-margin family (Section~\ref{sec:results-wagemargin}), and their finer skill-specificity measures offer a further robustness input.

The displacement treatment is static: displaced workers are fully out of work for the simulated year (earnings, hours, pension contributions and salary sacrifice zeroed, entitlements recomputed), while dual jobholders keep self-employment income. Unemployment duration is not modelled, so contributory-benefit exhaustion (new-style Jobseeker's Allowance lasts at most six months) is not captured, and over longer horizons I overstate support for displaced workers without means-tested entitlements. The sensitivity runs of Section~\ref{sec:results-aggregates} show the sensitivity to duration---the 50 per cent earnings-retention hybrid, which halves annual earnings while leaving status and hours at full-year unemployment, changes the fiscal cost from \pounds \genDurCentralCostMeanBn~billion to \pounds \genDurSixMoCostMeanBn~billion on the same draws---while the single 70 per cent take-up sensitivity gives \pounds \genTakeupCostMeanBn~billion. The wage-margin and mixed-adjustment exercises of Section~\ref{sec:results-wagemargin} relax the polar assumption that adjustment is either full job loss or pure wage cuts: holding gross pre-uplift earnings loss fixed at the paired draw's realised value, the fiscal cost changes from \pounds \genMixedZeroCostBn~billion to \pounds \genMixedOneCostBn~billion as adjustment shifts from wage cuts to displacement. Allowing the workers who retain jobs to lose wages rather than receive the assumed productivity uplift changes the result again: at fixed 7 per cent displacement, a 2.6 per cent survivor-wage decline gives a fiscal cost of \pounds \genSurvivorMinusTwoCostBn~billion and a BHC poverty change of $\genSurvivorMinusTwoPovBhcPp$ points. Ripple effects onto unexposed occupations are separately priced in reduced form in Appendix~\ref{app:ripple}: routing displaced labour supply to destination occupations and cutting non-displaced wages by an assumed inverse-demand slope of 0.3 raises the central fiscal cost to \pounds \genCentralRippleCostBn~billion. Because the slope is assumed rather than estimated, no conclusion that the central scenario is conservative follows; these exercises do not bound other labour-market responses.

The capital channel is narrow: it covers only interest and dividends, excluding rental income and returns inside private pensions. It is also sensitive to how much dividend income the data hold at the top. The processed FRS file used in the earlier version carried almost no dividends, so its capital channel ran almost entirely through savings interest. Microcosm is calibrated to SPI income by band up to \pounds 1 million of total income, its 2026 taxpayer dividend total is \genDivRatioAbsPct~per cent \genDivRatioDirection{} the SPI projection, and scaling dividends to that projection moves the central cost to \pounds \genDivSpiCostMeanBn~billion (Appendix~\ref{app:dataset-change}). Headline fiscal magnitudes should therefore be read as conditional on the dividend base as well as on the shock conventions. % results/robustness/dividend_coverage.json
Linked wealth data (the Wealth and Assets Survey) would sharpen it.

All scenarios exclude the self-employed from the labour-market shocks by design, following \citet{doorley2026}, because the exposure indices and displacement literature are not defined over them; since self-employment is a meaningful, partly exposed share of UK employment, this is a substantive omission.

Finally, the mechanisms invoked for some decile patterns---including savings buffers and income pooling within couples---are hypotheses consistent with the data, not formal decompositions. Across the scenarios examined, the tax-benefit system partly cushions household losses, and the extent of that cushioning depends on shock size, incidence and adjustment margin.
