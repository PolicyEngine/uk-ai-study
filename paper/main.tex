\begin{abstract}
\noindent I study how the simulated household and fiscal effects of an AI labour-market shock in the United Kingdom vary with its incidence. Linking occupation-level AI exposure to workers in Microcosm, a calibrated microdataset built from the 2024--25 Family Resources Survey, I use PolicyEngine UK to simulate employment, wage and capital-income shocks across 66 shock-size combinations and five displacement-incidence families that hold the expected number of displaced workers fixed. In the central 7 per cent displacement calibration, the mean Exchequer cost, measured as the fall in the government balance, is \pounds \genCentralMcCostMeanBn~billion a year, and absolute before-housing-costs poverty changes by $\genCentralMcPovBhcMeanSignedPp$ percentage points. Over 20 paired draws, the cost nets a displacement channel of \pounds$\genChannelMcDispCostSignedBn$~billion against the survivor-wage (\pounds$\genChannelMcWageCostSignedBn$~billion) and capital-income (\pounds$\genChannelMcCapitalCostSignedBn$~billion) channels, so it depends on the assumed capital-income uplift and on the dividend base; employer National Insurance contributes \pounds$\genCostEmployerNiSignedBn$~billion, and excluding it the central cost is \pounds$\genCostExclEmployerNiSignedBn$~billion (negative values are net gains). Incidence changes the fiscal result: for the same aggregate shock the mean cost is \pounds \genIncUniformCostMeanBn~billion under uniform incidence and \pounds \genIncKleinCostMeanBn~billion under a top-loaded stress test. The Gini rises in every displacement scenario examined. Shifting the same earnings loss from job losses to occupation-level wage cuts raises the fiscal cost, leaves the poverty change near zero and produces a smaller Gini rise. Both incidence and the adjustment margin change household outcomes and the public finances.
\end{abstract}
