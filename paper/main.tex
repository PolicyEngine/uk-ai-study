\begin{abstract}
\noindent I estimate how much of an AI-driven labour-market shock the UK tax-benefit system absorbs and who bears the rest. I link occupation-level AI exposure to microdata from the 2024--25 Family Resources Survey and simulate job-loss, wage and capital-income changes with PolicyEngine UK for \genModelFiscalYear. Displacing 7 per cent of employees costs the Exchequer \pounds\genIncUniformCostMeanBn--\genIncCompressionCostMeanBn~billion annually across four incidence rules. The same earnings loss through wage cuts costs \pounds\genWcPairCostDiffAbsBn~billion more but raises absolute poverty before housing costs by \genWcWagePovBhcMeanOneDpPp~rather than \genWcJobPovBhcMeanOneDpPp~points, because job loss concentrates losses on fewer households. With full Universal Credit take-up and Jobseeker's Allowance for the displaced, neither assumed above, job loss costs \pounds\genTkCentralJsaCostDiffAbsBn~billion more and raises poverty by \genTkCentralJsaPovBhcMeanOneDpPp~rather than \genWcJobPovBhcMeanOneDpPp~points. Costs are net of receipts from assumed survivor-wage and capital-income gains: the exposure-proportional cost, \pounds\genCentralMcCostMeanBn~billion, reaches zero if survivors' wages rise by \genBeWagePct~rather than 2.6 per cent.
\end{abstract}
