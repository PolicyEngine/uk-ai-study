\begin{abstract}
\noindent I estimate how much of an AI-driven labour-market shock the UK tax-benefit system absorbs and who bears the rest. I link occupation-level AI exposure to workers in microdata built from the 2024--25 Family Resources Survey and simulate job loss, wage and capital-income changes with PolicyEngine UK under \genModelFiscalYear~tax and benefit rules. With 7 per cent of employees displaced, the annual Exchequer cost runs from \pounds\genIncUniformCostMeanBn~to \pounds\genIncCompressionCostMeanBn~billion across four stylised rules for who loses the job, tracking displaced workers' earnings. Delivering the same gross earnings loss as wage cuts rather than job losses raises the cost by \pounds\genWcPairCostDiffAbsBn~billion but lowers the rise in absolute poverty before housing costs from \genWcJobPovBhcMeanPp~to \genWcWagePovBhcMeanPp~percentage points, because job loss concentrates losses on fewer households. These estimates keep each benefit unit's survey-drawn Universal Credit take-up decision and pay the newly displaced no contributory Jobseeker's Allowance; if every displaced worker's benefit unit claims and eligible displaced workers receive the allowance, job loss costs \pounds\genTkCentralJsaCostMeanBn~billion rather than \pounds\genWcJobCostMeanBn~billion and raises poverty by \genTkCentralJsaPovBhcMeanPp~rather than \genWcJobPovBhcMeanPp~points. Costs are net of receipts from assumed survivor-wage and capital-income gains: the exposure-proportional cost, \pounds\genCentralMcCostMeanBn~billion (50-draw mean), reaches zero (single-draw estimate) if survivors' wages rise by \genBeWagePct~rather than 2.6 per cent.
\end{abstract}
